VII. TRAVELING AND TRAVEL-EXPENSES (145-153)




145. Kt 91/k 424 (1-277-91)


A memorandum in the first person singular on travel expenses in copper for what seem to be two different trips. The first one (lines 1-20) presumably was a return trip from Wahšušana via Šaladuwar to Burušhattum; the second from Wahšušana via Tuhpiya to Durhumit and from there via Kanesh back to Wahšušana. The text was already used in  Geography, where lines 1-23 are quoted in footnotes 1243//1432  and 1479.

Notes
1. Little room for the missing first sign, probably 10 or ½.
7. Tarkua must be a personal name. I know of no other occurrences, but cf. Ta-ar-ku-a-lá in Kt 89/k 287:11.
12. Ušb/pug/kattum? is unknown from other sources. Reading adi ušbu ga-°me!-e¿r, “were spent while they stayed there,” is not supported by the traces and ana gamrim gamer is pleonastic.
19. See for nab/prītum, “paddock”, “stabling”, the note on *43:18.
22. The sign KIB with the value /dur4/ seems to be used here for /tuhx/. Its shape is similar to that in *146:23.25 (Dur4-hu-mì-id), which suggest they were written by the same scribe.  Forlanini 2012, 297 doubts whether the reading Tuhx-pì-a in our text, important evidence for its location between Wahšušana (which he would like to locate near Ankara) and Durhumit, is justified. The writing is indeed unique (the occurrence in Kt a/k 253b:27, mentioned in Geography footnote 1241, is uncertain and the context does not favor a place-name), but the reading seems probable, considering the occasional ‘creative’ use of signs by OA writers and because no town called D/Turpiya is known. The fact that expenses in copper from Wahšušana to what I take as Tuh/piya were 1½ mina of copper and from Tuh/piya until Durhumit twice as much, 3 minas, corresponds with the distances between Durhumit, Tuhpiya and Wahšušana, as drawn on Barjamovic’s map.
25. Note that in *146:24 too a payment of refined copper takes place in Durhumit, the centre of the copper trade.
Comment
Barjamovic in Geography uses our text as an important source for the reconstruction of the geography of the area east and west of the middle course of the Kızılırmak, with the cities Tuhpiya, Wahšušana and Šaladuwar. He assumes that Wahšušana is located east of the Kızılırmak and north of the Tuz Gölü, possibly at the crossing of the river at Büklükale, on whose lower terrace pottery sherds of the kārum level II type were found (personal communication M.T. Larsen). If so it is situated on the copper route that ran from Durhumit to Burušhattum, at the point where a crossroad east of the Kızılırmak and a road from Kanesh met (Geography 307 with note 1243, where he also adduces VS 26, 66:3-15; see the separate folding map “Anatolia ca. 1880 BC”, added to Geography). On p. 354f., with note 1479, he uses our text also as evidence that the copper road coming from Wahšušana passed through Šaladuwar (across the Sahiriya river) before reaching Burušhattum, located further to the south-west, possibly in the area east of Hittite Kuwaliya and north of the Sultan Daǧ, near Bolvadın (see his map 20). Forlanini 2012, rejects these last proposals (including the location of Durhumit) and we have to wait for a further evaluation of the facts, hopefully helped by new textual or archaeological data. During the recent excavations by Mrs M. Omura on the tepe of Büklükale, under some later deposits thus far only architecture from the Early Bronze Age has been exposed (personal communication). Excavation of the lower river terrace (mentioned above) might provide valuable textual evidence, to confirm or disprove Barjamovic’s and Forlanini’s proposed identification with Wahšušana or Durhumit.

Notes
1. I take buṭnātum as "pistachio trees", a word not yet attested in Old Assyrian. While its occurrence in Anatolia is not surprising (see now buṭnātim Burušhaddêtim in T. 125:16f., from Mari, cf. J.-M Durand, in Studies Veenhof, 2001, 130), the use of an Assyrian name is, since Assyrians normally use native Anatolian town names The writer may have translated an Anatolian name or he himself may have called the village after a prominent local feature, its trees. This might explain why it is not a real place name, but a designation of the type "the town of..." (āl ...), used in combination with a personal or divine name, a gentilic, or a noun referring to a specific local feature. Cf. āl Kupilšan (our text lines 17f.), āl I-a-ša (Ka 1113:4, usually contracted to A-li-a-ša cf. CCT 2, 39:26 and now R. Kuzuoǧlu, ArAn 7 (2004) 75ff.), āl ZI-ZI-im, "the town of ....." (RGTC 4, 7, with discussion), āl  dUTU, "the town of the sungod" (RGTC 4, 7), āl iṣṣurātim, "the town of the birds" (AKT 5, 56:3), āl tí-im-ni-e, "the town of ......." AKT 5, 20:18 and 21:16). The latter is perhaps a gentilic, comparable to ālum ša Hatti’ē (KTS 1, 8a:4f.), and especially to āl Kà-ni-ší-e/i, "the town of the Kaneshites", now attested in *149:14f. and *151:6. 
4. ni-IS-GA-ru, after ašar, looks a verbal form in the plural, cf. lines 7 and 16, but from which verb is unclear and one would expect vowel harmony.  
13. The purpose of the payment of 14 shekels of silver was omitted by mistake; the amount matches the cost of replacing a dead donkey. 
21. I follow Barjamovic’s reading Ta-e-dí-zi-na, cf. the writing of E in line 16.
30. šikku-copper is "a primary smelting product containing many impurities", whose value was usually half or one-third of that of fine quality copper, see Copper 42.

On p. 151 he calls our text “the most important itinerary for the location of Šamuha” and on p. 282 values it for locating Karahna “in precise relation to Šamuha and Kuburnat”.

One may compare Kt g/k 199:8 (Geography note 248), which first lists the road-tax (da’atum) for a journey from Assur via Hamizānum and Šimala to Zalpa, followed at the end by some additional expenses, comparable to those mentioned in our text, during the same trip, where Assur, Burullum, Hahhum and Zalpa are mentioned. See the discussion in Geography 95-98.


Two closely related records of travel expenses


The following two records, already published in Veenhof 2008b as nos. 2 and 5, concern shipments of merchandise belonging to Šu-Ištar and Iddin-Suen, who occur several times in records listing (shipments of) textiles (*316, *319, *317). In the account *320 Iddin-Suen is the owner of a shipment of nearly hundred textiles and the statement on how many of these were given as import-tax and pre-empted shows that they were imports that had arrived from Assur. The two records of travel expenses published here must refer to similar but bigger shipments, for the textiles mentioned in *320 imply a load with a value of only ca. 1 talent and 40 minas of tin. The lists of textiles, in which our two traders figure together with several others, indicate that these men had joined forces by entrusting their loads to a collective caravan (ellutum), whose leader upon arrival settled accounts with them for the costs of transport and clearance in the local palace on the basis of the ”declared value” of their share in the shipment.

General comments
Texts 146-151 were first published in Veenhof 2006 and Veenhof 2008b, to which *145, *152 and *153 are added here, not because they would be related to the other texts  - *147 may not even be from Elamma’s archive and the excavations numbers of 148-151 suggest that they were stored together, presumably by the same person, - but to present all such “itineraries” together. The first three and the last two deal with journeys inside Anatolia and *148 and *149 also cover the journey through Northern Mesopotamia, *149 from Wahšušana via Kanesh to Assur, and *148 the other way around, from Assur to Luhusaddiya. *150 and *151 cover a trip that started after crossing the Euphrates (where he have to locate the “Town of the Kaneshites”, see Veenhof 2006:22f.) and *152 and *153 deal with expenses during a trip in Anatolia. We do not know who traveled and wrote these memorandums (in the first person singular), hardly the same person in all cases (but see the note on *145:22). Some may have been written by Elamma or by one of his sons or servants, but 148-151, considering their excavation numbers, seem to belong to a file in which Enna-Suen, Šu-Ištar and Iddin-Suen play a prominent role. *150 and *151 were probably written by the leader of a caravan who charged its participants specified, proportional shares in the expenses of the journey.
        In Veenhof 2006 and 2008b I discussed the possible locations of the better-known towns and tried to reconstruct the routes followed. The data on Anatolia were taken up by Forlanini 2010:137-140 and I will not resume this discussion and refer the reader now to Barjamovic’s Geography, to whom I had made the records available before their publication. I only mention here that Forlanini (p. 138, note 21) does not accept the equation of Tapaggaš with Tapigga = Maşat Höyük  and so deprives us of a rare fixed point, but that Barjamovic maintains it (p. 258 note 987). Furthermore, that on 182 he makes the interesting suggestion that the “town of the Kaneshites” could have been “an idiosyncratic way of referring to the Assyrian settlement of traders at Hahhum, since it turns up in a position where one would have expected to find that city mentioned” - q.e.d. 
        Using such texts for geographical reconstructions, as pointed out in Veenhof 2006, repeated by Forlanini and amply documented by Geography, is difficult. They render account of expenses, so that places were nothing was paid are absent and they also do not mention how many days a trip or its stages took. Uncertain is also whether the sequence of towns reflects a linear journey or includes smaller trips from a "central place" to smaller towns in their neighborhood for commercial purposes This seems likely for the texts dealing with journeys in Anatolia, e.g. the return trip from Burušhattum to Ušb/pug/kattum in *145:11f., and for several small towns or villages visited according to *146 and *147. But some could be towns or villages along the caravan route, only mentioned when specific expenses were made there. The journeys recorded in *148 and *149, which ended in and started out from Assur, probably reflect linear caravan journeys, mentioning mostly well-known road stations on the caravan route (see for Hamizānum the note on *149:5). Birtu’um in *149:11 is thus far unique and was only mentioned because additional transport costs were paid for the trip from Burallum to this town. The trip of *153 was a short one by five persons in the north of Hattum, perhaps from Wahšušana, via Tawiniya and Tišmurna to Durhumit.         
        The terminology used for the expenses varies. Most frequent is “I gave”, usually with the purpose (ana) of the payment (porters, personnel, inns, officials; an unknown “him” in *152:18-22), but we also have “I incurred” (ikšudī and ikšudanni), usually not specified (but in *147:24 said of the wages of the porters; note for the impersonal ikšudam in *149, AOATT 413ff.), and occasionally “I weighed out” (*151 passim), also in the D-stem (*145:4, and *151:5, 20). The first two texts also use “I spent / ... was spent” (agmur, gamer) and the noun gamrum, usually specified as “(for the journey) until GN”. These are unspecified “travel expenses” (gamrum ša harrānim, see AOATT 248-53), which could include extra food, pasturing, additional transport costs (taššiātum), crossing a river (*146: 22), using a bridge (*147:10), etc. In addition there are payments of taxes, the šaddu’utum due to the colony in Burušhattum, paid in silver (*145:9-11), and the import-tax paid to local authorities, normally in towns with an Assyrian commercial presence: 6 shekels of tin on textiles in Hanaknak and 3 minas of refined copper for the kaššum in Durhumit (*146:10 and 24), 6 shekels of silver paid to ....., probably in Kuburnat (*147:34f.). *152:21-22 mentions “his road-tax” together with fodder for the donkey”. The “gift” of 12 shekels of tin to the “commander” (rabi sikkitim) in Karahna (*147:19f.) may also have been a kind of transit-tax. Such payments were probably based on agreements with the local rulers whose towns were passed, whereby instead of a tax in natura (e.g. a piece of textile) also tin or silver could be paid.[4] A regular feature are gifts to local chiefs and officials, such as the bead of tin for the alahhinnum (*146:20), but such persons are regularly designated by the generic term kaššum, “official, dignitary” (*146:19, 25), either because the writer did not know his title or considered it not important.[5] 
        It is interesting to note where silver, tin or copper was paid, because in different areas, depending on the composition and provenance of the caravan, different types of "money" were used. In *145 most payments were in copper, apart from that to the colony and the inn in Burušhattum. The caravan of *147 (arriving from the south) pays tin (presumably the “loose tin” taken along from Assur for expenses en route), also the small gift (12 shekels) to the commander in Karahna, but silver is used for the import-tax of l. 25 (in Kuburnat?). In *149:19 and 23, and *150:5-13 payments in southern Anatolia were made not only in tin (with “tin for in the hand”), but also in silver, presumably because one wished to spend silver that was becoming “cheap” in Anatolia, while the exchange value of tin increased there, from ca. 1:15 in Assur to eventually ca. 1:6-8 in Anatolia. The beginning of *152 mentions the sale of a few textiles for silver, which was then used to buy copper, and all the following expenses are in silver. In *153, which records very small payments, some were in copper, but on various occasions one also paid very small amounts of  silver (presumably also in line 1, as da’atum), while a servant received both some silver and some copper  for his expenses (lines 11-13). 
        At times the size of the expenses may give a hint of the distance covered, e.g. in *145, where most expenses for the trips from town to town amounted to 3 minas of copper, but the short journey from Wahšušana to Tuhpiya required only 1 ½ mina of copper and the long one from Durhumit to Kanesh 5 minas. During the trip from Kanesh to Wahšušana also 3 minas of copper were spent, but here “small wares” (lines 28f.) too were used for paying. Occasionally porters were used, in *145 during the first two stages and in *147 to reach Šamuha and for the trip from Karahna to Kuburnat. Perhaps more difficult terrain was passed, a donkey had died or additional merchandise was transported. That donkeys were used is shown by the cost of nabrītum (*145:19), “stabling”, a place where donkeys could stay and graze (see OAI, 267f.). Payments to inns for accommodating persons and donkeys (lodging, food, water) are well documented (see Geography 34-37). Their use in towns with Assyrian trading stations (Burušhattum, Hurama, Šalatuwar, Wahšušana) may indicate that their premises did not offer visiting caravans sufficient facilities for passing the night and guarding the load, because they were not walled compounds or the caravans were too big. An inn offered both, cf. AKT 6, 560:22-24, “we gave 3 shekels of silver to the inn in Qaṭṭarā, where one guarded the goods” (ana bēt ubri ša Q. ša unūtam iṣṣurūni).
