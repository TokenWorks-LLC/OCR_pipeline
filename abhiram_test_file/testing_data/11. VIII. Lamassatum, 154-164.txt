VIII. LAMASSUTUM (154-164)




VIII. LAMASSUTUM (154-164)


        Elamma’s wife Lamassutum – also written Lamassatum, without vowel harmony– is well attested in the archive, both during and after her husband’s lifetime and she also occurs in some texts found in other archives. Kt 94/k 543:4-5 mentions: “1 mina 10 shekels of silver / under my seals to Lamassutum / the wife of Elamma; 3 shekels of silver / a consignment for the same woman” (šēbultam aššiātima), entrusted somewhere in Anatolia to Ellil-bāni, presumably for delivery in Kanesh. There are  several references to her as sender of small amounts of silver to Assur, especially to her daughter Ummī-Išhara (see Ch. II, B). She also figures as writer and as addressee of some letters exchanged with relatives and we meet her in contracts, because she  conducted some business of her own, both before and after her husband’s death.
        A few records of Lamassutum were found among the texts of what must be Ir’am-Aššur’s archive (see Ch. XI, introduction). They are a debt-note for ⅔ mina of silver, which a certain Dadiya owes her (dated only by a double week eponym; Kt 94/k 120), a contract with Iddin-Aššur about the conditional sale of his daughter Ab-šalim (or a loan with his daughter as pledge, dated to eponymy year 100; Kt 94/k 127), and a damaged agreement between her and Ilī-bāni on a sum of silver (Kt 94/k 123). The archival context of these records and the documented family relations suggests her identity with Elamma’s wife. Lamassutum also figures anon-ymously as “the wife of Elamma”, e.g. in Kt 93/k 73:11 (see Appendix text 23f).[1] In other cases the identification is uncertain, because Lamassutum, “guardian angel”, was a popular name for girls. According to Michel (2008, 60f. with note 32) also a sister of Elamma’s father had this name and she or a namesake must have written the letters CCT 4, 40a, KUG 39 and Prag I 467 from Assur to Pūšu-kēn and Imdīlum. She also may have been the one who received the small shipments of silver recorded in CCT 6, 27b:9 and TC 3, 207:26. And if she was identical with Elamma’s aunt she was different from Lamassutum, daughter of E-ra-dí (Prag I 569:8). The identity of the Lamassutum attested in EL 180, AKT 1,1:3 (during eponymy year 88) and AKT 6, 12:4’, and of the one mentioned in AKT 4, 45:6 and 20 (probably the sister of Uṣur-ša-Ištar) has to be established by more prosopographical research.


A. Records on slaves


        Three records deal with the sale of the slave-girl Kukra. *154 is an agreement between her and Šamaš-abī, that he will pay her within ten weeks the rest of the price for Kukra; if not the slave-girl becomes her property. *155, designated as “tablet concerning Kukra of the son of Zuzānum” (=Šamaš-abī)”, records a testimony given under oath by four witnesses at the behest of the Durhumit colony, but since the envelope was not opened we do not know what they testified on. *156 is a deposition by the same four witnesses, also summoned by kārum Durhumit, in which they declare that the slave-girl “of Šamaš-abī, son of Zuzānum” had been bought by Šallim-Aššur, son of Iphurum. This tablet could be a copy of the tablet inside the unopened envelope *155, but its implications are not certain, because damage of the bottom of the tablet deprives us of five or six lines of text and the envelope has not been opened.

Comment
A reconstruction of the affair is difficult, also because the two depositions are undated. I assume that Šamaš-abi, son of Zuzānum (who occurs in Kt 93/k 9:13-14 - courtesy of C. Michel - as a transporter and as witness in AKT 6, 838:5, undated) had bought the slave-girl (the seller may be mentioned in *156:2-4, see below) with money advanced by Lamassutum, part of which -  20 minas of refined copper, equivalent to ca. 20 shekels of silver - he failed to pay back. Later he may have sold the slave-girl to Šallim-Aššur, apparently without having paid back Lamassutum. She then must have summoned him and her claim may have led to a lawsuit before the colony, in the course of which *155, a deposition by the four witnesses, played a role. In *156 they confirm that the slave-girl really had been sold (again) by Šamaš-abī, but we do not know how the case ended. It is difficult to consider the agreement *154 the result of the intervention by the colony, because it is not presented as its verdict or as a solution by mediators or persons “who solved the case” (pāšir awātim). Moreover, if the slave-girl had been sold to a new owner Lamassutum could not stipulate that the girl would become her property if her new owner defaulted, unless this sale was contested, because the seller could not sell his encumbered property. The kārum may have decided that the sale by Šamaš-abī was not valid, because he still owed part of the price of the girl to Lamassutum, but such a verdict is missing. It is more likely that *154 documents an early phase of the transaction, which established Šamaš-abī’s debt and stipulated what would happen if he failed to pay in time. One could then assume that the defaulting Šamaš-abī, instead of handing over the slave-girl to Lamassutum, had sold her, perhaps even in order to use the price paid for her to pay Lamassutum. That the debt-note *154 was found in Lamassutum’s archive does not contradict this, because the tablet may have been preserved after its sealed envelope had been broken.

Comment
The description of the girl sold as “the slave-girl Kuzizi” indicates that the Anatolians did not sell their daughter into slavery, but a slave-girl they already owned, presumably to meet a debt-claim. Nevertheless, the sellers retain the possibility of buying her back, which they can do by declaring “We take her along”, using the verb tarā’um that is typical for such situations (see Kienast 1984, 74ff., on the “Retraktklauseln”, in the combination šaqālum + tarā’um). They can get her back by paying twice the sale price, which makes it difficult (since they presumably are already in debts) to make use of their right (see Veenhof, Studies Matouš II, 295ff. and R. Westbrook, Property and the Family in Biblical Law, Sheffield 1991, Ch. 5, on the “price factor” in redemption). Lines 19-21 consider the possibility that the sellers (try to) take the girl along without paying and state that she then becomes the full property of the buyer, as a chattel slave. Cuneiform law does not have an abstract definition of “ownership”, but stating, as in our contract, the right to sell what has been bought (which implies that redemption is then excluded) is its nearest approach. Cf. in Old Babylonian CT 4, 7a, a dispute about the ownership of a house acquired by exchange, where one party declares (lines 25-32), “He gave me the full price of my house and a house in exchange for a house, so that I am satisfied. .... I can give it where I wish. What claim do you have one me?” In our contract the possibility of selling the girl may not only have been an expression of full ownership, but have been intended by Lamassutum, if the slave-girl was merely sold to cover a debt and she wished to see money.
        Protection against vindication by a third party is provided by stipulating that the buyer in that case will make the sellers with their property (their house) liable for it, for which the verb dagalum, “to look at, have a claim on, to acquire ownership of” is used (see Veenhof 2001, 129, 3). In this clause, as in the one about attempts by the sellers to recover their property, the buyer is speaking and the writer of the record quoted what Lamassutum said verbatim, as happens more often, especially when mentioning specific securities claimed by the buyer (or creditor); (cf. *154:12-13)

Lamassutum must have been producing textiles in Kanesh, presumably woolen pirikannū-textiles, perhaps with the aid of her slave-girls (*164:23 mentions five of them), when her husband was still alive. Lassen 2010, 165, mentions a few other texts in which Lamassutum is involved with wool, but I am not certain that it is always the same woman, because the name was rather common in Old Assyrian. Lamassutum in our contract needs soft wool for making the weft. To obtain this type of wool she contracts (and presumably provides money to) Aššur-taklāku, who has to send it from Haqa, situated north of Zalpa, on the road from Assur to Tegarama (Geography 111). It may have been available there thanks to imports from the north, from Luhusaddiya or the region around modern Çorum, a centre of wool production and trade (see Lassen 2010, 168f.). Aššur-taklāku may have passed Haqa on his trip to Assur, and if he fails to carry out his commission and travels straight to Assur, he has to supply double the quantity contracted as a penalty. That the debt-note was preserved unopened in Elamma’s archive might indicate that the commission was not carried out.

Ir’am-Aššur was married to Lamassutum’s daughter Šalimma and there are several references to Ah-šalim, a well-known name, perhaps the son of Buzutaya (*204:11), who also acted as transporter for Elamma (*82:5) and received and owed silver in *117:3 //*310:4 and *90:4.10.18 (accounts settled with him according to *85:15’f.) and some textiles in *317:6.
25. Ilina, daughter of Aššur-ṭāb, occurs only here and may have been a granddaughter or a grand-niece (if this Aššur-ṭāb is the son of Elamma’s brother Ali-ahum) of Lamassutum, who had somehow deserved her gratitude.
28-30. It is not clear whether all three men or (which seems more likely) only the third, Maṣi-ilī, was Lamassutum’s representative. The latter could be the son of Buzuzu (*173:10, *180:18) and must be the man who also figures in the records dealing with the aftermath of Ištar-lamassī’s death (*185:6; *177:28). He may have been employed by Elamma, for according to *39:27 he had received a “working capital”. We encounter Ištar-pālil, presumably the son of Šu-Kūbum, as debtor of Ali-ahum, Elamma’s brother, in *300:5, 21. There are too many different Ennam-Suen’s to identify him, but he may well be the man of that name many of whose records, edited in this volume, were found in Elamma’s house (see Ch. XIII).
35. The title of Lamassutum’s daughter, NIN.DINGIR, the OA equivalent of ugbabtum, is sufficient to identify her without mentioning her name.
Comment
The assets Lamassutum left behind were substantial: silver objects, cash silver and debt claims on various persons amounting to in all 8⅔ minas of silver, plus ten textiles and ten slaves and slave-girls. The origin of these assets is not mentioned, some may have belonged to her dowry or have been given to her by her husband (e.g. the silver cups and toggle-pins), but since he died several years earlier she may also have obtained some as her share in his inheritance (wives did inherit from their husbands, see Veenhof 2012, 176-7). The temple funds (ikribū), put at Elamma’s disposal (lines 5-6), may have been passed on to her in this way and possibly also some of the other claims listed in lines 6-14. Other assets were debts owed to herself, resulting from her commercial activities, as documented in the debt-notes published above, and from the fact that she had made amounts of silver available to Assyrians, among them her relative Ir’am-Aššur, “for (to make) purchases” (lines 15-19), probably in Assur. But this expression is also used in l. 22, where sale in Anatolia of textiles “given” to Pilah-Ištar (presumably her son) is meant. The nine pirikannu-textiles he received in fact may have been woven by her or her slave-girls, for in *159 she commissioned a trader to buy wool for her.  Lamassutum also owned quite a number of slaves, perhaps including some she had bought herself, such as the one recorded in *157.
        That her representatives had to entrust “all this” for shipment to Assur may imply that outstanding claims had to be collected and the slaves were sold (apart from one), to send their yield in silver there. But we cannot exclude that eventually some debt-notes were assigned to her sons, to be collected later in Anatolia. That the final accounting and division of the inheritance would take place in Assur – where also Lamassutum’s sons had to go for this purpose (lines 31-33) and where Ummī-Išhara lived as priestess - is known from other texts. The final injunction, “My daughter ... and my sons shall act in accordance with the testamentary dispositions applying to them”, obviously quoted from her testament, might reflect some fear about the execution of her last will, but it may also have been fairly standard clause. Other testaments show that “executors” (bēl šīmātim) would monitor the division.
