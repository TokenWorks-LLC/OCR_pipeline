IX.  THE CHILDREN OF ELAMMA (165-178)






IX.  THE CHILDREN OF ELAMMA (165-178)


As pointed out in the Introduction Elamma had a large number of children, in alphabetical order (we do not know their chronological order): Abu-šalim, Aššur-ṭāb (probably his eldest son), Ennam-Aššur, fIštar-lamassī, Pilah-Ištar, fŠalimma, Šu-Bēlum, fUmmī-Išhara and Uṣur-pī-Aššur. Not all of them are well attested and several figure in records that have found their place in other chapters, such as the one on Ištar-lamassī (X) and Šalimma (XII), and children also figure in the texts in the chapter on Lamassutum (VIII) and Ir’am-Aššur (XI). This chapter brings documents that have not found their place in these chapters. 


A. Letters

Comment
This letter was sent from Kanesh, where Lamassutum lived, to the place where the main addressee, Šamaš-bāni, stayed. It must have been delivered by Pilah-Ištar (see l. 19), who also brought the missives of his brother Ennam-Aššur along from Kanesh, as his mother there announced, perhaps including the transport contract drawn up for this lot, which then would have mentioned the witnesses (here already named in lines 13-16) present when Ennam-Aššur entrusted this lot to Aššur-šamšī. Why the “stupid” Aššur-šamšī refused to release the textiles is unknown, but the letter makes it clear that the textiles, shipped under the seals of Ennam-Aššur (l. 6), had to be handed over to the addressees in order to be “counted out” to Pilah-Ištar, who probably would have to sell them. He was ordered “to act in accordance with our instruction” (l. 28), an instruction not contained or repeated in this letter, but presumably given him when he left Kanesh.

Notes
25-26. Barjamovic, who collated the tablet, in Geography 171 proposes AN.NA li-°ší-ib¿-ma and translates: ”leave the tin to them and have it placed all over the packs for [...] (tucked) inside the wool and the hides”. But the traces - ŠÍ-IB looks more like RA – do not support this reading and wašābum only occurs with animate subjects. Comment
The letter is interesting in showing how traders might try to maximize their profit. A donkey loaded with tin and some textiles, having crossed the Euphrates, has entered Hahhum, from where it will travel into Anatolia (the use of “to bring down”, šērudum, is remarkable for such a journey)[2] to sell the goods. The writer suggests to sell the tin locally if they can get 1 shekel of silver for 8 shekels of tin, which is a modest yield, less than the ideal of 6:1, but acceptable in the south, for it saves the trouble of the journey northwards, which implies that the writer means a sale in or around Hahhum. If they cannot get that price the merchandise has to be “shipped on” (šētuqum) and there seem to be two possibilities, which in the interpretation of Geography 191, are: via Šalahšuwa presumably on to Kanesh  (from where the letter may have been written), or via Timilkiya on to Durhumit, presumably via Kuburnat. In the latter case a more profitable solution is to let “people from Kuburnat” – probably located ca. 100 km. south(east) of Durhumit – if they are around, take the tin along, hidden in their loads of wool and hides (which they had acquired in the south). For that purpose small packets (riksū) of tin have to be made, which means that the donkey was unloaded and the large packets (šuqlum) were broken up into smaller ones. This interpretation is suggested by the letter BIN 4, 48 (see AOATT 312 no. 9), where riksu’s of 10 or 15 minas have to be made and hidden in loincloths to be smuggled into a town, exactly the same procedure as suggested in CCT 6, 22a:8-10. This interpretation suits the idea, also entertained by Barjamovic, that one will travel north via the “narrow track”, which starts north of Hurama, a way of evading taxes with amounts to smuggling.

B. The death of  Elamma’s son Aššur-ṭāb


The following three texts deal with the death of Elamma’s son Aššur-ṭab (also clear from *174:27-28, where his son Aššur-imittī figures alongside Elamma’s widow and his other sons in a legal confrontation). The texts document how his nephew Aššur-ṭāb dealt with the assets he had left behind and how the dead man’s representatives, two brothers and his brother-in-law, monitor what happened. According to *171//172 the latter had summoned him and asked before witnesses what he had done and would do and in consequence of an appeal by them the Burušhattum colony had made these witnesses testify. This indicates that the affair had been brought before the colony, but nothing is known of its outcome.

Comment
This contract can be linked with Prag I 609, which explains what had happened with Elalī’s trust: “As for the tablet about 8 minas of silver plus the interest on it, which amount Elalī, son of Ištar-pilah, had given as trust (iqīpu) to Ennam-Ašsur, the deaf (sakkukum), in Hahhum (5) and for which he had drawn up in Hahhum his certified record, which Išme-Aššur, son of Hinnāya has in possession – (10) I, Ēnah-ilī, Aššur-taklāku and Puzur-Ištar, son of Imdī-ilum, each took 2 minas of silver and supplied (15) a sealed quittance, which Iliya, son of Puzur-Aššur, has in his possession”. This shows that the affair was even more complicated. The original debt-note was in the possession of a son of Hinnāya - why is not clear, perhaps simply as safe deposit, because he lived in Hahhum? - and four different traders, among them our Ēnah-ilī, subsequently had each acquired 2 minas or one fourth of the original trust, a fact recorded in a tablet (singular!) they had sealed (lines 14-15, apparently one tablet for all four jointly), but their relation to the original trustee remains unclear. Who the speaker (“I”) of Prag I 609 was is not clear, because we do not know from which archive this memo originates. It would be nice if this was Elamma, because it explain why Ēnah-ili, a trusted agent of him, had collected the silver «in his name», after Elamma's death, to give it to his widow, but this would imply that the division of the trust into four, recorded in Prag I 609, had not yet been realized when Elamma died. An alternative is that Ēnah-ilī in Prag I 609, in Hahhum, already acted as agent of Elamma, so that his action described in our record amounts to acquiring Elamma's share, apparently in the meantime augmented by 15 shekels as interest. Many things remain unclear, because it is not stated what the purpose of the original trust given to Ennam-Aššur was, although it seems likely that the silver was the value or price of merchandise arriving from Assur in Hahhum, which had to be shipped on and sold. We also do not know why this trust was subsequently divided into four; could Puzur-Aššur could not handle it or was he in financial problems? The division reminds me somewhat of the transaction described in text 2 of the Appendix, where 4 talents of tin of Hinnāya are divided in his house into three, apparently to traders who intend to sell it with profit. It is anyhow very likely that Elalī in the meantime had died, because lines 21-22 consider the possibility that investors (was the money of the trust perhaps part of his joint-stock capital?) and the (other) sons of Ištar-pilah might raise claims. The purpose of our record is to ensure that the heirs of Elamma, who had received his share, protect Ēnah-ilī against their claims and against those of the original trustee, Puzur-Aššur. The existence of both our record and Prag I 609 show how complicated financial transactions carried out in subsequent stages cold be and how we may be puzzled or misled if we happen to have only one record from what must have been a larger file.

Comment        
This record deals with the division of some of Elamma’s assets between three of his sons and his daughter, Ummī-Išhara, who lived as ugbabtu-priestess in Assur, during the year REL 115, long after he had died and also after the death of their mother. The other two sons – Aššur-ṭāb had died in the meantime - Abu-šalim and Uṣur-pī-Aššur were probably not involved, because this is an exchange of parts of their shares between four heirs only. But they are meant by “the (other) sons of Elamma” who might raise a claim (lines 38-39). Ummī-Išhara and Ennam-Aššur (who lived in Kanesh, but may have liked to have a pied-à-terre in Assur) wish to acquire a house-plot in Assur, measuring ca. 100 m2, presumably still undivided and owned by the four of them, and in exchange for it they waive the right to their share in debt-claims probably inherited from their father, totaling 12 minas of silver (lines 4-9). Their two brothers, Pilah-Ištar and Šu-Bēlum, are ready to “drop the (right to) their part” and give them the plot, but only after that property has been cleared, because they wish to be protected against future claims of the investors. The clearance (tēbibtum) according to l. 21 must be effectuated “for them”, for these two brothers, within three years. If they fail to do so, Ummī-Išhara and her brother will get 3 minas of silver instead and give up any claim on the plot.        
        Lines 13-14 mention that the house plot in question “had been given to them (presumably the four children) by a committee of five” (hamištum). It probably was involved because the implementation of Elamma’s last will created problems, since the house-plot was encumbered or its ownership disputed. Elamma’s investors could have established their claim at the time of Elamma’s death, in order to secure the return of their investments, a concern also clear from the letter CCT 5, 3a (see Appendix, text 1), where two groups of people state that they are acting in the name of Elamma’s investors. Why and how such a ”clearance” (tēbibtum; first occurrence in OA) could prove impossible (line 22) remains unclear. That the last line (an appendix, added after the names of the witnesses) states that the transaction was “in accordance with what had been assigned to them in the testament” (of their father), suggests that what is at stake here is a reshuffle or exchange based on what had been assigned to each of the heirs in the testament, a feature also known from contemporary Babylonia. This could also have been the reason for the involvement of the “committee of five”, for we have more evidence for its role in arriving at a fair division of an inheritance. In EL 244 two sons of Pūšu-kēn will go to Assur in order to take cognizance of the decision taken for them by a committee of five there, “behind the temple of the god Aššur”, because “the decisions for/about us” are in the city” (lines 9-11). The two brothers must have disagreed on the division of their father’s inheritance and what the committee does is called šīmtam / šīmātim šiāmum, which is the terminology of making a testament, not that of a normal legal decision or verdict. The decisions (šīmātum) taken by the committee are comparable to what a man does who makes a testament and they may have been needed because there was no testament of Pūšu-kēn.[4] Something similar may have caused the intervention of the hamištum in our text.
        U. and E. are promised 3 minas of silver (to be taken from the 4 minas of Kurub-Ištar, mentioned in lines 5f.), if they fail to acquire the house plot, to “satisfy themselves with the price of the plot” (lines 22-26). This would be the value of half of the plot, on the assumption that Ummī-Išhara and her brother, as heirs, already were the owner of half it. Until the matter is settled P. and Š. retain their claim on these 3 minas, which means that its ownership by U. and I. for the time being was conditional. A problem is why lines 4-8 list claims to a total of 12 minas of silver (half of which would have been the share of Ummī-Išhara and Ennam-Aššur), while the compensation for the house-plot to be acquired by the two other brothers is only 3 minas,  to be taken only from the 4 minas owed by Kurub-Ištar,
        This record also acquaints us with the rather surprising fact that many years after their father’s death four heirs still jointly own undivided assets, not only a house plot in Assur, but also various debt-claims, which have not yet been collected, presumably because they were not simple debts, but investments. It is possible that the death of their mother, several years after their father, is part of the explanation. But in a family of traders the division of an inheritance, consisting of various assets, investments, debt-claims and houses, could be complicated and take time. This is also clear from the following document that deals with a small amount of silver.

Comments on *176-178
The main person in our texts, Uṣur-pī-Aššur,[6] is a son of Elamma, but he appears only here and we know nothing about him. He also does not figure in *178, where four children of Elamma reach an agreement about dividing certain assets of what they had inherited. Also mentioned are Elamma’s daughter, Ummī-Išhara, in Assur, and his son Ennam-Aššur, living in Kanesh, who was the one in direct contact with his brother. It seems that Ennam-Aššur and Uṣur-pī-Aššur at this time were the only children of Elamma present in Kanesh, while the others were in Assur (cf. *177:11-13).
        Our texts concern three issues, the payment of silver to Uṣur-pī-Aššur by his brother, the fact that Uṣur-pī-Aššur had as wife a slave-girl, presumably from the household of his mother, and that Ennam-Aššur had made some payments for his brother in connection with a slave of the latter, who had fled and which Ennam-Aššur managed “to get out of” (šēṣu’um) the house of an Anatolian.
         The issue of the slave remains unclear. The verbs “to hold” (ka’’ulum) may imply that Uṣur-pī-Aššur detained the slave as pledge or security, either supplied a by defaulting debtor or perhaps a defaulting debtor himself, sold into debt-bondage. This could explain why he fled, but how and why he ended up in an Anatolian’s house is unclear, also because the verb šēṣu’um is used in different situations and may denote both that a pledge was taken along from a defaulting debtor by his creditor and that somebody obtained the release of such a pledge from the house of the creditor.[7] Had the Anatolian apprehended him and did he receive a reward or had he himself a claim on the man and did Ennam-Aššur pay him to get him free? 
        The colonial verdict *177, possibly the result of an appeal by Uṣur-pī-Aššur, shows that there had been legal problems, for it states his right (on which it was based remains unknown, because lines 3 is broken) to live in and from the house(hold) of their father on the same footing as his elder brother, and to keep a slave-girl he had taken as wife, but forbids to alienate any property until Elamma’s other children, in Assur, have reported on the actual (legal) situation.
        Texts *176 and *178 begin with the issue that Ennam-Aššur in Kanish had given silver to his brother and I assume that the 15 shekels given according to *176 are identical to the 15 shekels borrowed from a money-lender at interest according to*178. He may have done so to prevent it from  being formally recorded in a dated debt-note. Text *176 suggests that Uṣur-ša-Aššur had claimed the silver as part of the inheritance of their mother, but the gift of was conditional and *176 (drawn up in Kanesh by or for Ennam-Aššur, who could not ascertain there the details of the division) states that Uṣur-pī-Aššur will give it back if his sister in Assur can produce (?; the verb is missing) a valid record by which Uṣur-pī-Aššur had renounced any further claims on the inheritance. What he had already received is unknown. *164 registers the assets left behind by their mother Lamassutum, which will be shipped to Assur, and at the end quotes words from her last will: “My daughter ... and my sons will act in accordance with the testamentary dispositions applying to them”, but we do not know the latter. But the slave-girl Uṣur-pī-Aššur had taken as wife seems to have been one of the five owned and left behind by his mother in Kanesh, one of which she had already assigned in her last will to her grand-daughter Ilina (lines 23-25). I assume that Uṣur-pī-Aššur, perhaps with the silent consent of his mother, some time earlier had married (*177:8-9 uses the verb ahāzum) one slave-girl as concubine or wife and that the Kanesh colony decided that this “acquisition” was an accepted fact (kīma ahzuni ahiz). He could not be forced to give her up, so that she would become an asset of the inheritance still to be divided, but the verdict (also referred to in *178:14-16) stated that he could not convert her into money by selling her (*177:9-10). 
        There is more evidence of the role of slave-girls as part of an inheritance. The agreement recorded in EL 287:18-32 states that, after the eldest(?) son has received his share in silver, the others will “divide the rest, barley, slave-girls, slaves and bronze objects (read sí-pá-ri!) in accordance with the testament of their father”. One son will take “here (in Kanesh) or in the City (Assur) a slave-girl as hierodule”(? ana ištariuttišu); three others “will each take one slave-girl from those with whom they had already intercourse (ša lamdū) and she will be deducted from their share”. The last will Kt o/k 196c (I. Albayrak, ArAn 4 [2000], 22f.), after assigning one slave-girl to the testator’s daughter (l. 15), in lines 23-27 first prescribes that the testator’s wife and sons “shall divide the silver, slaves and slave-girls that remain on equal terms” (“half half”, mutta muttat), but adds that “Aššur-idī’s concubine is relinquished to him” (šawissu ša A. waššurassum). This must mean that he can keep her and perhaps that this girl is not included in the assets to be divided, which would make this provision similar to the one in our text. *178:14 describes the slave-girl married by Uṣur-pī-Aššur as “belonging to the girl”, which probably means that she actually was part of the share assigned to Ummī-Išhara. If this is correct then the fact that Uṣur-pī-Aššur could keep her was a favor and the no-claim promise given to his sister (*176:6-15) could have been the result of accepting this favor. 
        Text *178:17-19 describes how this affair ended, apparently stating that Uṣur-pī-Aššur somehow got rid of the girl (the verb is unfortunately unclear) and left, perhaps frustrated by the decision which prevented him for selling her. I hardly have to add that this reconstruction is hypothetical, because we are not informed about how this case ended and know little about the elusive Uṣur-pī-Aššur.
