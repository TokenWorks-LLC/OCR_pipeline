X.  IŠTAR-LAMASSĪ  (179-188)
    


 X.  IŠTAR-LAMASSĪ  AND HER DEATH (179-188)
        
        The group of records presented in this chapter deals with events surrounding the death of Ištar-lamassī. She was a daughter of Elamma, as is stated in Kt 94/k 181 (courtesy C. Michel), where she seals an undated contract in which her sister Šalimma (the wife of Ir’am-Aššur) buys a slave. She was originally married to the Assyrian Kūn-ilum, with whom she had two sons, Iliya and Ilabrat-bāni, and a daughter, Šīmat-Ištar, who lived in Assur as ugbabtu-priestess. After Kūn-ilum’s death she married the Anatolian Lullu, son of Hapi, and ten years later she died, almost immediately followed by the death of both her sons, which left her daughter in Assur as only heir. In the absence of sons, who would have been responsible for her funeral and the rites surrounding it, relatives and her second, Anatolian husband stepped in. He advanced the costs of her and her sons’ funeral, but had to be indemnified (also for money he had earlier advanced on behalf of Iliya), which was done by taking the money from what Ištar-lamassī’s sons would have inherited according to her last will. Relatives, Elamma’s widow Lamassutum and his eldest son Ennam-Aššur, together with the representatives of her daughter, Šumi-abiya (called her šazzuztum) and another Ennam-Aššur, and probably the men who had witnessed her last will (as its executors?) arranged this and ultimately sent what Šīmat-Ištar had inherited and what remained from the shares of her dead brothers to Assur. 
        These operations generated a number of records, such as letters sent to Assur to relate what happened, records of the transfer of money and assets, and accounts of the expenses and payments made, which turned up in Elamma’s archive and provide us with a unique file. Unfortunately, there is no detailed report on the find-spots of the texts, whose excavation numbers suggest that they (or many of them) were found together and had been kept as a file. I studied this file in “The Death and Burial of Ishtar-lamassī in kārum Kanish” (Veenhof 2008a), where texts *180, *184, *185 and *188 were already published. The present edition contains a few minor improvements in the reading of the texts, due to collation and the general comments at the end aim at a better understanding of the structure and meaning of the file and the function of the persons involved. I refer to my previous study for various details, especially for what these texts learn us about the funerary rites, such as the “bewailing” of the dead and the removal of the chair of Ištar-lamassī. In the comments below I focus on the features that are relevant for the family history and the financial operations that conditioned how much of Ištar-lamassī’s testamentary disposition could be realized.

Notes
13-17. Twice a house “is held”, apparently as a pledge for a debt claim and I assume that “their tablets” are the contracts recording this fact and that the debts were owed to Ištar-lamassī or her husband. The testament assigns them most probably to Iliya, for his short name fits the gap and there follow items belonging to “both of them” (both brothers jointly).
18. The reading lu-q[ú-tum] is tentative and difficult in view of the verbal predicate in the masc. plural (izzazū) and because the father of the boys was long dead, but I see no alternative, also not when second sign is restored as MA or even DU. The reference must be to non-perishable items that had become Ištar-lamassī’s property and were now bequeathed to her sons. 
21-23. The woman who is the subject of šabbu’at, “she is satisfied with .....“, and presumably also of talaqqe is unknown, one would assume that it was Šīmat-Ištar, but there is no room for her name or title (gubabtum). There could be a connection with *181:2, where the silver Šīmat-Ištar gets from her mother derives from the latter’s bābtum, “outstanding claims”. The qablītum that Iliya acquired was probably a kind of metal bowl, two of which, each weighing 1 mina (of silver), were owned by Ištar-lamassī according to *186:4-5 (in l. 18 they occur alongside a mirror). 
28-29. The three men probably were also the executors (bēl šīmātim) of Ištar-lamassī’s testament; we meet them in other records of the file (*182:15-18, *185:1-6, *186:30, *187:1).

181. Kt 91/k 425  (1-277-91)


The continuation of a letter to Šīmat-Ištar (see lines 8-9 and 16), which starts by quoting what the writers had told the persons (presumably creditors) who threatened to take the silver Ištar-lamassī had left for her daughter. It then reports how this claim was refuted and gives details about the financial arrangement and what was sent to Assur. Finally it mentions the presence of un-opened containers with tablets of Ištar-lamassī and her sons and warns Šīmat-Ištar that, due to her mother’s second marriage, she must not expect to receive much silver.

General comment
While what happened after the death of Ištar-lamassī and both her sons is in the main clear, problems of interpretation remain. The identity and role of most of the persons involved in the affair is understood, especially of those protecting the interests of Ištar-lamassī and her children, also the writers of most of our records. They are relatives, Lamassutum and her son Ennam-Aššur, representatives of Ištar-lamassī’s children, Ennum-Aššur, son of Aššur-dan and Šumi-abiya, son of A/Emua, and probably also those who witnessed Ištar-lamassī’s last will and may have been its executors (bēl šimātim), and perhaps also a few more people. They probably are the ones designated as “our brothers” (184*:12), who entered the house for the funerary rites and they must belong to the group to which *181:15 and *185:12 refer, mentioning actions ina puhrini / ippuhrišunu, “of us/them jointly”. Those trying to get at the money Ištar-lamassī left behind, apart from Lullu, are elusive, “the son of Hinnāya, creditor of your brothers”, who tried “to seize” it (*180:36-37), and anonymous persons who wished “to take it for the debt of her (Šimat-Ištar’s) brothers”. We also do not know who “the sons of Hanum” are, protected by *187:16-17 against any further claims of Lullu. 
        Uncertainties are also due to the nature of the texts. Some are clear, such as the (unfortunately damaged) testament *179 and the two transport contracts *182-183, which have legal force. They were written in view of the shipment of the money that remained to Assur; the first records that it was handed over to the two representatives of the children, the second  how these men sealed it and entrusted it to a transporter. Text *186, a witnessed protocol about the use of the assets of Ištar-lamassī, whose speaker and addressee remain unidentified, has legal force, as does *187, an unopened sealed envelope that contains a witnessed record establishing that Lullu has been indemnified for all expenses paid for his dead wife (“the lady”) and for Iliya (when he was in jail) and renounces (he has sealed the envelope) all further claims on the sons of Hanum. Text *180 is a letter sent to Assur, which ends fairly abruptly, and *181 apparently is the continuation of a letter, but not of *180, because it starts by quoting what the writers (“we”) had told persons who wished to take what Ištar-lamassī had bequeathed to her daughter. The latter is the main addressee of both, as is clear from *180:5ff. and *181:8. Found in Elamma’s archive they must be archive copies or perhaps drafts that remained in Kanesh. Text *184 is a memo written by the same “we” (l. 12); it mentions what was taken or paid (the main item are the costs of the funerary ceremonies; the verb is an impersonal passive, “it was paid”) from what Ištar-lamassī had left to her sons and ends with the statement that what remained of it “will go to their sister” in Assur, of which the two representatives of the sons will take care. Text *185 is an memo recording that eight persons, including the two representatives, made (and paid) the expenses for the funerary rites “from the silver that was in the house of Elamma’s wife” and that the representatives had sealed the containers (hušālū), apparently holding the relevant tablets (for shipment to Assur, together with the silver for Šīmat-Ištar, or for safekeeping in the archive?). One of these tablets might have been *186, a memo whose writers (“we”) specify how much silver was paid from what Ilabrat-bāni had inherited for the (presumably his) funerary ceremony (bewailing, meals and drinks on two days); after deduction only 4  7/12 shekels remained of his share in the cash.
        The sequence of the events and some texts raises problems. We do not know when Ištar-lamassī made her testament, but apparently there was time for her son Iliya “when he was still alive”, as mentioned in *180:5-7, to “take 7 shekels of silver from his share”, after his mother had “given” it to her children and when the money had already been deposited in Elamma’s house. This letter explains that, after opening her sealed “coffer” (tamalakkum) Ištar-lamassī “gave” her children their shares in her cash money on her deathbed, in the presence of the “we” and three affiliated traders (mer’ū  ummiānī; perhaps the witnesses to the testament). From lines 10-11 (“we entered with your mother”) we get the impression that she was lying ill in her own house, where also her coffer was, and it seems likely that it was afterwards transferred to Elamma’s house (did Iliya take a part of his share before or after this?). The agreement between the division of the cash money as reported in *180 and mentioned in the testament *177 could mean that she implemented her existing last will on her deathbed, but it is also possible that she decided on the division of her cash money then and there, so that the written testament was the result of the formal gathering at her deathbed, when “she spoke her word” (*awassa taqbi). This expression is used twice for making one’s testamentary dispositions (in which the testator normally speaks in the first person singular) and in AKT 6a-44 is such an oral declaration in the presence of witnesses (see Veenhof 2012a, 170f.). The subsequent transfer of her money coffer to Elamma’s house (where also her last will was found) may have been a safety measure, perhaps because she was not certain about what her second Anatolian husband would do. 
        The division of Ištar-lamassī’s cash money is clear from her last will (*179): of the 57 shekels of silver available (in her coffer, *180:12) Iliya received 37 shekels, Ilabrat-bāni 20 and her daughter 7½ shekels plus 2¼ shekels of gold (equal to ca. 20 shekels of silver) and also her mother’s seal. The same figures are found in *180:18-23, but here her daughter gets 8 shekels of silver. What was eventually sent to Assur to Šīmat-Ištar are 2¼ shekels of gold and 37 shekels of silver (*181:10-11, *182:1-2) and this last sum consists, as *183:1-2 shows, of the 8 shekels assigned to her by testament plus 29 shekels. This last amount is presumably what remained from her brothers’ shares after deduction of expenses for the “bewailing” and for paying a debt owed by Iliya to Lullu. Text *180:35 mentions that these together amounted to 27 shekels and *184:16-18 that they included the 7½ shekels paid for Iliya’s debt, so that the “bewailing” of the mother and her two sons cost 19½ shekels, indeed the figure given in *184:13. After deduction of these 19½ shekels from the total of 57 shekels available according to the last will, there remained 37½ shekels for Šīmat-Ištar, which is very close to what *181:10-11 and *182:1-2 state and identical if we assume with the last will that not 8 but 7½ shekels were originally assigned to her. 
        It is not clear how the expenses for the bewailing of Ilabrat-bāni and the accompanying funerary meals, recorded in *188 and amounting to 15 5/12 shekels of silver, fit into this picture. Are they included in the 19½ shekels of *184:13 and do we have to assume similar expenses for Ištar-lamassī’s and Iliya’s funeral or was there one funerary ceremony and meal for all three? The question is also what we understand by bikītum, “bewailing”. Was it exactly what the word means, which may have implied the hiring and perhaps feeding of wailing women, or a more general term for the whole funerary ceremony? *181:7-9  speaks of expenses (only) “for the “bewailing of your mother and your brothers”, but *184:9-11 mentions “the bewailing of them and the removal of the chair  of their mother”, a combination also used when *185:13-14 mentions the expenses made for it. Memo *188 not only makes a distinction between expenses for “bewailing” (l. 3) and those for food and drinks “on the day of the bewailing” (l. 7), but states that the latter are paid cash (lines 10, 23-24), while those for the bewailing are “booked” (lapātum), apparently to be accounted for later. 
        What Ištar-lamassī and presumably her sons left behind was more than cash money. Lines *179:13ff. of the last will mention “tablets”, presumably recording debts for which houses were held as pledge, and various metal objects which a woman (presumably Šīmat-Ištar) and Ištar-lamassī’s two sons would get. In *186:1-3 we read about a share (an asset recorded) in a tablet and various valuable objects (a few of which are also mentioned in *179: 23-26), all of which were entrusted to somebody for paying the debt owed by Ilabrat-bāni to Lullu. We do not know the origin of these assets, they might reflect Ištar-lamassī’s own commercial activities, but also be what she had inherited (long ago) from her first Assyrian husband, who was engaged in trade, as we know from Elamma’s archive (cf. *81:30-31, *121:5-6), property which she must have kept for herself after her second marriage. *181:23-26 mention that also two sealed containers (ṣiliānū) with tablets were deposited in Elamma’s house. They may have contained the tablets mentioned in the last will (*179:17), the tablet mentioned in the memo *186:1-2, 23, and records that document the ownership of the house and textiles mentioned there in lines 16-17. The “silver from outstanding claims” (bābtum) left behind by Ištar-lamassī, mentioned in *181:2, must also have consisted of debt-notes and other records of claims (perhaps the tablets of *185:17), which the heirs could collect. Finally *186:16-20 mention “the price” of a house,[2] of textiles and of various precious objects, part of which would be used for paying the debt of Ilabrat-bāni.
        It is important to distinguish between what the sons and what Šīmat-Ištar had inherited and what was used to pay the expenses. *181:4 reproaches anonymous persons, presumably creditors (cf. *180:36-37), of trying to take what was bequeathed to Šīmat-Ištar “for the debt of her brother(s)” (a-HI-ša can also render ahhēša, plur.), but their attempt was frustrated. Memo *188 indicates that Ilabrat-bāni’s funeral was paid from his share (qātum) in the cash money as mentioned in the testament (*179:8-9) and his debt to Lullu would be paid by using assets of Ištar-lamassī (*186). The situation of Iliya, the eldest son, is more complex. He had been assigned more cash than his brother, 37 shekels of silver, but had already appropriated 7 shekels of it (*184:5-7). Moreover, he owed his father-in-law Lullu (*180:27-29) “the expenses for the jail” (gamrum ana kišaršim) the latter had paid for him after Iliya had spent all he had, a debt that amounted to 7 ½ shekels according to*184:17. How much he acquired as heir (apart from his own earnings, for he was involved in the trade, as the nishatu-debt owed to the līmum-office, mentioned in *181:18-21, shows) and what he owned at the time of his death is not clear. He had not only obtained some cash silver, but also the debt-notes mentioned in *179:17 and one qablītu-bowl (lines 23-25), and he may have been entitled to a share of Ištar-lamassī’s assets when they were sold (*186:16:29). His sudden death leaves these questions – at least for us – unanswered and we know nothing about the commercial activities of him and his brother. But their and their mother’s death were instrumental in generating an in many ways unique file, that sheds light on a little-known aspect of the life of the traders.
