XII.  ŠALIMMA (206-210)


 XII.  ŠALIMMA (206-210b)


        According to Kt 93/k 513:17 (courtesy of C. Michel) Šalimma was a daughter of Elamma;  in *206:23 she calls Elamma’s wife and his sons “her mother and brothers”. She was married to Ir’am-Aššur, son of Id(d)i-bāni (see for him Ch. XI), as we know from *206, which deals with the problems between them (see below and Veenhof 2007b).[1] Since Elamma’s son Ennam-Aššur in *190:34 addresses her husband as “my father, my lord”, he probably was somewhat older and this might apply to Šalimma too, since according to *208 she had two grown-up sons, Šu-Ištar and Aššur-imittī, active in Kanesh, to whom she writes a letter from Assur. She may have been Elamma’s oldest daughter, perhaps even a daughter from a first marriage, but there is no proof for that. She could have been the mother of Ir’am-Aššur’s daughter Suhkana, married off in Kanesh to a certain Aššur-mālik according to Kt 94/k 149 (edited by Michel-Garelli 1995, 298f.), and this Suhkana then could have been one of her “children” (šerrū) mentioned in Ummī-Išhara’s letter *206:31-33. But Suhkana may also have been a daughter from an earlier marriage of Ir’am-Aššur. 
        The house in which Šalimma lived in Kanesh must have been the one of her husband Ir’am-Aššur, also after the latter had returned to Assur and she had refused to join him, as *206 tells us. When she eventually returned to Assur her two sons may have continued to live in it, as suggested by *208, unless they moved to the house she bought according to *210. We would expect records that belonged to her to have turned up in her own house, from which presumably the first group excavated in 1994 (Kt 94/k 114-187; see Ch. XI, introduction) in another part of the lower town originates. The information provided by C. Michel reveals that this group of texts includes a debt-note (Kt 94/k 137) in which Šalimma is the creditor of three Anatolians (Dalaš, Kikaršan and Taliya) for an amount of 15 shekels of silver, and a contract (Kt 94/k 181, an unopened envelope) about the purchase by Šalimma of a boy from an Anatolian man (Dušala) and his two sons. The presence in Elamma’s house of the letter *206, addressed to her and her mother Lamassutum, if not one of two copies, is not surprising and this obtains also for the letter once encased in the envelope *207. The presence in Elamma’s house of *209 and *210, in which Šalimma is creditor and buyer, and of the letter written to her sons, *208,  may be due to what happened after her return to Assur, but we have no information on it. The family ties between Elamma and Ir’am-Aššur may also be responsible for the presence of a few records of members of one family in the archive of the other, such as some documents of Lamassutum in Ir’am-Aššur’s archive (a debt-note of her, Kt 94/k 120, and a contract with Iddin-Aššur about the conditional sale of a girl, dated to eponymy year 100).[2] 
206. Kt 91/k 385 (1-239-91)


A long, emotional letter sent from Assur by Elamma’s daughter Ummī-Išhara to her mother Lamassutum and in particular to her sister Šalimma in Kanesh, on the problems the latter is causing by her refusal to join her husband Ir’am-Aššur in Assur, of which Ummī-Išhara, due to her efforts to mediate between the two, is becoming the victim. I have edited this remarkable letter, with a long commentary, in Veenhof 2007b, to which I refer the reader for detailed comments. The assumption that she did return in the end to Assur is perhaps confirmed by her mention in Kt c/k 30+:16-17 (courtesy Dercksen), a list of amounts of silver sent to Assur: “10 shekels (of silver) a gift/consignment (šēbultum) for Šalimma and Masātum”.

Comment
What is readable shows that this is not part of the envelope of the letter  206*, as I assumed in Veenhof 2007b. This is confirmed by the presence of two different seal impressions, observed by Mrs Omura, of which one belongs to Ir’am-Aššur. The presence of this envelope in the archive suggests that the document was broken due to the damage suffered by the house, but I have been unable to find the letter it must have contained.

Notes
A:1’. At the end of the line we need a verb with grain as object, either šaqālum (surprisingly used in B:8), madādum, tadānum or wabālum. 
A:3’- 4’. A clause stipulating which weight is to be used when paying back (not written in B), occurs more frequently in debt-notes, see AOATT 57f. and Copper, 80-89. Comment
The Anatolian couple probably had become indebted by a loan or by the obligation to deliver goods already paid for and the debt rated in silver is to be paid back in grain. The fact that payment in silver (called “to give back”, ta’’urum) is only an alternative shows that the goal of Šalimma’s transaction was to obtain grain, presumably not for commercial purposes, but for her own stocks. Another contract that offers such a choice is Kt d/k 20B:13-16, concerning a silver debt of 45 shekels, “If they give (back) barley, it will be deducted from their account according to the current rate of exchange”. This implies that the 6 sacks of line 4 are the equivalent of the 15 shekels of silver, which yields an acceptable price of 2½ shekels of silver for one sack of grain.[3] I thus assume that lines 4-7 do not state the amount of interest to be paid, “6 sacks of grain (uṭṭutum), 1½ of which are wheat”, but that the main sentence consists of l. 4+8a: “6 sacks of grain ..... they will pay at harvest time”, into which the stipulation about interest is embedded, where the use of the verb šaqālum in l. 8 reflects that what the debtor had received was silver. The use of ina ŠÀ.BA, “thereof”, to denote the interest on a debt is strange and unique.[4] ŠÀ.BA is used to specify the composition of an amount of goods, e.g. that an amount of grain to be delivered must consist of two different kinds, barley and wheat - hulled barley and bread wheat, best attested among plant remains from Central Anatolia from this period [5] - and it seems possible that the writer of our contract was influenced by it. The amount of interest, 1½ sacks of wheat, considering that wheat is often 50% more expensive than barley,[6] would equal an interest of at least 2½ sacks of barley or ca. 40 to 50%, which is an acceptable rate. It is a pity that little remains of the text on the envelope, which might have given a clue. The obligation, mentioned after the payment clause, that they will give 10 breads as “additional gift”, is a well-known feature, studied by Matouš 1965, 180f., "Exkurs über Zugaben".
        The due date for the expected payment in grain is harpū, the summer, the time of the harvest; that for payment in silver some months later, at the festival of Anna, in autumn, probably to leave the debtors some time to acquire silver by selling part of their harvest. This seems to be a more general pattern and for examples I refer to OBO 160/5, 242f., where the due date for grain is also designated as “at harvest time” (ina ebūrim) and “on the threshing-floor” (ina/ina erāb adrim), and that for silver also as “when the grapes are plucked” (ina qitip kerānim),[7] which means in early autumn.

Translation
“Seal of the Kanesh colony. The colony passed the following judgment: Amur-Ištar will swear by Aššur’s dagger (6) that he house has truly been bought with Šalimma’s silver, that he (tablet adds: his heart) honestly did not know that the silver belonged to Lamassutum. (10) If Amur-Ištar swears the house belongs to Šalimma. If he does not swear Lamassutum will swear by the tambourine(?) of Ištar (15) that the house was not bought with Iddin-Suen's silver, but was bought with her own silver. If she swears the house is her house. Should Amur-Ištar and Lamassutum (20) (both) not swear but reach a compromise, the colony will take 4 minas of silver from the price of the house.


Notes
The scribe consistently used the sign LA1 and wrote Lamassutum's name with vowel harmony.
6-8. As libbušu shows, the oath, in the subjunctive, is not quoted as direct speech, but as a dependent clause, without introducing conjunction. 
15. We already knew that a woman does not swear by Aššur's dagger, but by the hoop (huppum) of Ištar, from Kt 94/k 131: 8-13 (see Michel 1997c, 111-112), ”If it is a male by Aššur's dagger, if it is a female by Ištar's tambourine(?) one will swear”.
19. The use of the singular here shows that the plural bētū in lines 5, 11 and 15 also refers to a single house.
Comment
This verdict may well concern the house bought by Šalimma from her sister and brother according to *208. The names of the persons mentioned in the verdict may support this view, for Amur-Ištar could be Elamma's brother of that name and Iddin-Suen the father of Elamma and Amur-Ištar. The ownership of the house depends on the question whether it was paid with the buyer's private money or with money «of the family» (inherited from Iddin-Suen). That Šalimma herself is not present and does not swear suggests that she had died (in Assur, where she had returned?) and that the ownership of the house had to be established before it could be inherited (by Amur-Ištar?). Amur-Ištar first gets the opportunity to swear to defend his case and it is remarkable that he not only has to confirm the purchase with Šalimma's money (as he no doubt had claimed), but also his honesty by denying any knowledge of what the other party has claimed. If he fails (refuses) to do so the other party is not automatically granted the ownership, because there are still two possibilities: purchase with the money inherited from the pater familias (which must have consequences for the inheritance) or with Lamassutum’s private money. Unfortunately we do not know what was the result of the oath procedure.
        If both parties at the last moment - after the colony had convened and the verdict had been issued and before the oath ceremony- reach a compromise, the colony will be indemnified for the trouble they had caused it by a heavy fine to be paid by the parties. This heavy fine probably served as a deterrent, which is understandable in view of the great number of legal cases to be tried by the colonial authorities. Parties are warned to consider carefully whether they are going to make an appeal. In our case they might consider whether the price of an Assyrian house in the colony was high enough to run that risk.

In TC 2, 53:1-16 wheat plus barley were purchased for on average 7 shekels of silver per sack and sold at double that price, followed by cases where persons “took” barley or wheat for much lower prices. In ATHE 12:22-25 the price of wheat was even 18 shekels per sack (4½ shekels per jar). In ICK 2, 2:2'-4' wheat was ca. two and a half times more expensive than barley (“30 sacks of barley instead of 12 sacks of wheat”), but in Kt 88/k 71:15-17 (see AoF 35 [2008] 97) the difference was only ca. 50%  (barley at ca. 1 1/6 shekel per sack, wheat ca. 1 2/3 shekel) and in *343 barley cost 3 shekels per sack, wheat 5 shekels.
