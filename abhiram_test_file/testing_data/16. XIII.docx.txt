XIII. ENNAM-SUEN (211-234)




XIII. ENNAM-SUEN (211-234)


        The house of Elamma contained about thirty texts related to a certain Ennam-Suen, letters written to and by him (alone or with others), legal documents, in which he is the key figure, as party in a conflict, as guarantor, and as creditor or debtor. In most cases the name of his father is not mentioned and the assumption that these texts concern the same man rests for several of them on their subject matter and the persons involved, and for all of them on the fact that they were found in the same house. As their excavation numbers, ranging from Kt 92/k 94 to 187, show (only *228 was found in 1991), they were discovered in 1992, according to Tahsin Özgüç’s letter to me “collapsed on the floor of the south side of the second room”. He adds that “they enabled us to describe the presence of a new Assyrian merchant”, which most probably (he does not mention a name) refers to Ennam-Suen. The 1992 group, however, also comprises a fair number of records of Elamma and Lamassutum (letters, memorandums, etc., mainly those with excavations numbers between Kt 92/k 142-162), which suggests that there were (at least) two different groups or files, probably arranged as such on shelves, which were mixed up due to their collapse on the floor. Data on the remains of containers or exact find spots to confirm this idea are not available.  
        Ennam-Suen was a very popular name and we know some forty different men so named. Only two texts of our group give the name of his father, Amur-Šamaš in *232:4-8, where Ennam-Suen and his younger brother Āl-ṭāb appear as debtors of an unnamed creditor, and in *233:7-8, a quittance for a debt Ennam-Suen owed to Ilam-kurub.[1] Their subject matter does not link up with that of the other texts, but if Āl-ṭāb was indeed the brother of “our” Ennam-Suen, it becomes understandable why he acted as his guarantor in *226 during eponymy year 82,[2] which is only one year later than *232. The dated occurrences of Ennam-Suen in our file, always in debt-notes, eponymy years 82 (*229), 92 (*230), 96 (*231), and 99 (*228), have a time range which is entirely possible for one single trader. Moreover, the excavation numbers of the two texts mentioning his father, Kt 92/k 163(*233) and 164(*232) link them with other ‘Ennam-Suen texts’, which makes it likely that the same Ennam-Suen, son of Amur-Šamaš, is meant in all of them. While the quittance *233 would normally belong to the debtor’s archive, the debt-note *232 in its envelope would be expected in that of the creditor. 
        An Ennam-Suen occurs four times in the lists of textiles published in Ch. XX, regularly together with Iddin-Suen, Šu-Ištar, Aššur-rē’ī and a few others, the first two of which also feature among his correspondents in the letters published in this chapter. This suggest that the Ennam-Suen in both groups is the same person. In the comments on the textile lists I argued that they probably deal with substantial lots of textiles belonging to traders who had cooperated in a joint caravan (ellutum), which arrived in Kanesh and were cleared in the palace. The Ennam-Suen of this chapter probably is also the one who in *219:9-10 (a letter of Aššur-imittī to Ennam-Aššur) is involved in the settlement of a problem concerning a lot of textiles. Other occurrences of an Ennam-Suen in texts of the 1992 group, without mention of his father’s name, such as *245:8 (witness to a seizure by Šu-Ištar of silver belonging to Bēlum-rabi), do not allow conclusions on his identity. In particular not because in our group we twice meet a different Ennam-Suen, who is the son of Aššur-ṭāb. In *279:17-18 he testifies before the Wahšušana colony about a settlement of accounts between two traders, and in *284:16-17 he witnesses Ṭāb-ṣilli-Aššur’s “seizure” of Šu-Suen.
        No person with the name Ennam-Suen occurs among Elamma’s relatives or his well-known business partners, investors or agents, but in the texts excavated in 1991 at least five Ennam-Suens occur:
E. son of Ennam-Aššur, witness in *298 during the year REL 108. Outside the archive as         witness also in Kt 91/k 127A:1 (an undated quittance) and AKT 6-b, 489:2. According to         TPAK I, 156 he was redeemed from an Anatolian (creditor?) by Šuppianiga, whom he has to         serve for the next five years. 
E. son of Ikua, in *228, during the year year REL 98, is creditor of two traders, who owe him 2         minas 44 shekels of silver. He is witness in AKT 6-a, 251:32 and involved in a conflict with         Ilabrat-bāni during the year REL 91 in Kt a/k 977.
E. son of Kurkurānum, in *49 sealer of an unopened envelope that contains a tablet about the         “declared value” (awītum) of merchandise of him, Susāya, son of Isihum, and Elamma,         which reflects the shipment of their goods in a joint caravan (ellutum).
E. son of Sukkaliya, acts as witness in *57:3, a debt-note probably from the year REL 88,         recording a claim of Elamma on Ilšu-rabi. In KTK 79:4 = EL 66 his son Aššur-mālik         figures as creditor in the year REL 106.
E. son of Šu-Ištar, occurs in *195:22, a letter of Ir’am-Aššur, in which he mentions tablets of his         recording his indebtedness to Innāya, Ennam-Suen, son of Šu-Ištar, and Aššur-mālik.[3] He is         the best known Ennam-Suen, who functions twice as week-eponym in year REL 108, and         probably as year eponym in REL 115, and who appears as a trader who transports silver and         merchandise (ATHE 19:4-5; Ka 1004:18-19, šēp E.), and as creditor (TC 2, 16:4).
It is impossible to identify one of them as a man whose close relationship with Elamma could account for the presence of his tablets in Elamma’s archive. Witnessing a debt-note may be due to a relation with its creditor, but not necessarily so, and cooperation in a joint caravan (ellutum), “an organizational form for joint conveyance of merchandise” by “groups formed on an ad hoc basis” (OAI 167f.), does not prove a lasting partnership. This applies also to *53, whose anonymous writer (who could be Elamma) states that he will collect an amount of silver as “one third share (šalšātum) in the caravan of Ennam-Suen”.
        Most other occurrences of an Ennam-Suen in texts from Elamma’s archive also prove little. In *209:4 he is witness for a debt with Šalimma as creditor, in *251 he acts together with Muti-Aššur as judge in a conflict between Aššur-mālik and Šū-Bēlum, and in *76:10-13 // *77: 5-10 (see the comments on these texts) we meet an Ennam-Suen indebted for two amounts of copper to the anonymous writer of these texts. Most promising is *164:28-30, which mentions that Ištar-pilah, Ennam-Suen and Maṣi-ilī as Lamassutum’s representatives will see to the conveyance of what she bequeathed to Assur. This responsible task suggests a relationship, in particular with Elamma’s widow, who still lived in Kanesh. A hypothetical explanation, if our Ennam-Suen was the son of Šu-Ištar, is that at some time, perhaps when he went back to Assur to become līmum in year REL 115, boxes with tablets of his were deposited in Elamma’s house (ana nabšêm ezābum), but this is difficult to prove. We can only study the texts as they have been preserved. They show him to have been an active trader, especially as owner of lots of textiles, and it is interesting that he occurs in these records together with Iddin-Suen and Šu-Ištar, who also figure in his other texts, notably in letters to and from him. The dates of these and other texts in which he occurs range (if indeed all concern the same man) from the year REL 82 to the years after Lamassutum’s death, which anyhow makes it impossible to identify the just mentioned Iddin-Suen with Elamma’s father of that name.

3. The usual meaning MAŠ = tū’imum, “twin” (logogram and syllabic writing alternate on CCT 6, 1a, tablet and case) does not fit, because this designation is used either alone (Puzur-šadu’e DUMU Tū’imim, CCT 2, 44b:5; TC 3, 99:21; CCT 6, 1a:2) or in apposition to a personal name (e.g. TPAK 1, 179:21-22), never preceding one.
6. It is not clear who of the three addressees is the main one addressed here.
20. The past tense tal-qé is strange after ušerradamma and must be a mistake for ta-lá-qé.
22-23. This is an informative description of a judicial oath procedure, designated by the words ana mamītim palālum + personal object, which uses a verb restricted to OA (CAD P, palālu B; see now OALP 364-367) and only attested in such contexts. The occurrences show that palālum could affect a party, but also the witnesses he might summon. In our text palālum was the initiative of a man who tried to force his opponent to accept this procedure. In the somewhat similar verdict TTC 4 = EL 339:10-12 a formal interrogation leads to suing one’s opponent: “The attorney of P. and P.’s representatives will .... interrogate E. and he shall answer them (10) and where they are going to sue (him) they will palālum” (a-le / iše’ūni / ipallilū). RA 88 (1994) 122:30f., shows that this leads to an oath, “where we will palālum the attorney must make (him) swear an oath” (ašar ni-pá-li-lu rābiṣum / lutammi). The question remains whether this, expressed in our text by palālum ana mamītim, is (has to be) always the case.         See also Veenhof, Lex., s.v.
        Our letter mentions two possibilities for the palālum procedure:
a) The plaintiff writes a letter in which he states his grievances to his representatives in the place where his opponent lives, they can make him swear, in which case the resulting sealed tablet (with his testimony or acknowledgment) must be returned to the opponent's repres-entatives, in the place where the plaintiff lives. 
b) If this does not succeed, the plaintiff must “force the opponent to move” (nasāhum, Š-stem), to come to where the plaintiff lives, where he will swear the oath (lūmā). The writer adds lines 31-32, “I will come if you take responsibility and then we can discuss the matter there” (where you live). He uses the stative of the verb maṣā’um, typical for expressing that a plaintiff, in an appeal to the authorities, is ready to accept the responsibility and bear the costs of the legal action undertaken, especially the forced transfer of a person who has to appear in court (cf. already OACC 257-259). The oath sworn (l. 30) is not the end of the proceedings, for it can be followed by a discussion (atwûm) on the basis of the contents of the oath and the facts so established, to solve the problem. 
        The wording of the oath was carefully formulated in advance, to cover the relevant issues and prevent a verbal escape, as shown by records that start with the statement: “Listen oh god, lord of the oath” (e.g. EL 284 = CCT 5, 14b). In Elamma’s archive *287, a sealed, unopened envelope with the inscription “This is the tablet of the oath of K. son of A.”, must have been such a case.[4] I assume that in our letter the suggestion to write down “the grievances” in a letter had a double purpose: to state that and why the accused has to swear and to record the issues at stake, so that the representatives could carefully formulate what he had to swear.
Comment
Was the writer, called Šarrum-kīn, whose name is preceded by MAŠ, perhaps the (later) king Šarrum-kīn, who as prince as some time would have been present and active in Anatolia, in Wašhaniya? The issue of the letter is a conflict of the writer with one of the three addressees (“you”, l. 6), who accuses him of having removed textiles from the house of the writer (l. 12). The writer rejects this accusation and states that the (local Anatolian) palace is responsible for it, during his absence. He mentions that it had used the service of four Assyrians, “affiliated traders” (lines 9-14), who had searched his house and removed the textiles. This is a remarkable procedure, which implies that the palace had a valid claim and had decided (or was authorized?) to realize it by using Assyrians, perhaps because they could read the sealings on the (bags with) textiles, to identify those of the man they were after. The writer is ready to provide his opponent with a written statement (“tablet”) to that effect (l. 20), presumably (a copy of?) the testimony recorded under oath by these four witnesses, mentioned in lines 19f. If that is not enough he is ready to submit himself to a formal oath procedure, which should clear the facts and allow to solve the matter in a discussion (atwûm, l. 32).


Three letters from Šu-Ištar: “Get rid of the maid!” (215-217)


        The following three letters – the third one could be a second page or a letter without proper address – all deal with the same issue, a maid (amtum) who is wicked (raggat) and  contracts debts, of whom the writer, who stays elsewhere, wants to get rid (halluqum). He describes what she has done and asks Ennam-Suen, alone in *217 and together with Kukua and Uṣur-ša-Aššur in *216, and presumably also in *218 (address missing), men who live in Kanesh (“there”) where the maid also lives, to sell her. But not to people of Kanesh and Assyrian traders, but to foreigners, men from Talhat, a town across the Euphrates in Northern Meso-potamia (see Veenhof 2008b, 18ff.).[5] In this way she will not turn up again in Kanesh (*216:29) and her buyers must be instructed not to bring her back (*218:19). 
        We do not know who the maid was. Considering her role (see *217:13-15) and the fact that in *217:18 she is designated as awīltum, probably the Anatolian wife (for whom the designation amtum was used) of the writer, who is called “her lord/owner” (*216:15), a term that does not necessarily mean he owned her as a slave, because bēlum is also used for the husband who is his wife’s “lord”. Šu-Ištar may have acquired her from her parents by paying an amount of silver (some marriage records - AKT 1, 77:18, TPAK 1, 161A:1, and  Kt n/k 1414:6 - do mention the payment of a bride’s “price”, šīmū). While selling a slave-girl was her owner’s right, selling one’s Anatolian bride would have been unacceptable, unless the girl’s misbehavior might have been a sufficient reason for doing so. Marriage contracts mention such a “misdeed” (šillutum, Kt 91/k 132:13 and Kt d/k 29A:10, not necessarily restricted to sexual misbehavior) and the latter stipulates that in that case she is sent away with only a single toggle pin (ina ištêt tudittim, l. 11) to fasten her garment. The best parallel to the treatment of the maid in our text is provided by Kt 94/k 141, in which an Anatolian girl married as amtu-wife will be “stripped of her garment and cloak and sent away naked(?) if she is false or commits any act of impudence”.[6]  Because these contracts mention sending her away, but not the right to sell her into slavery, the relation between maid and “lord” may have been different in our contract and I also note that the sale to foreigners looks like a questionable deal (one might translate halliqāši  also by “make her disappear”), which has to carried out by cheating her.
        Šu-Ištar is probably identical to the man of that name who occurs in several lists of textiles, where also Ennam-Suen figures, and he could be the one who has a legal fight with Ennam-Suen in *224. Our three letters exhibit a few peculiarities, such as the plene writing of the pronominal suffix (*216:15.20.23; *217:12; *218:6), the use of TA for dá, and ahhū’a attunu written with crasis as a-hu-a-tù-nu *(216:11 and *218:1), while *216 and *217 spell qibima in the address with the sign bi2, which is uncommon. These features may be due to Šu-Ištar, if he himself wrote these letters that deal with a rather personal affair.

19. I take the verbal form as imperative of the Š-stem of waṣā’um, rather than as one of šasā’um, “to call, settle accounts” (as abbreviation of nikkassī  šasā’um).
20. The adverb e-ni-ma-ma also occurs (see Grammar § 15.11 with note 699) in AKT 6-a, 257:3, e-ni-ma-ma našpertam .... ukallimka, where Larsen translates: “now here”. In our text a temporal meaning “just now”, “quite recently”, or the like would make sense. 
21-25. Aššur-imittī apparently was the boss, perhaps the head of the firm or a main investor (in *219 he warns Ennam-Aššur not to quarrel with his brothers). Aššur-šamšī is in a difficult position, because the local Anatolian palace (in Wašhaniya, l. 9) is angry with him (lines 23-25), which presumably means that he has an unfulfilled obligation. He asks Ennam-Suen for clemency, i.e. not to press his financial claim, inspired by the latter’s sister, and tells him that he has also begged Aššur-imittī for help. To express this he uses the idiom ana šēp PN maqātum, not yet recorded for OA in CAD, but now several times attested, see my note on Prag I 467!:23 in JAOS 122 [2002] 799. He asks him to make precious gifts to appease (ṭa’’ubum, or perhaps to satisfy)  the palace, but Ennam-Suen is unable to do much, because (lines 32-33) he also has a substantial debt to the palace. 
27-31. The meaning of these lines it not very clear to me. “Seizing witnesses” usually means making them testify to confirm or deny a claim. It seems that Aššur-šamšī wants to hear from Ennam-Suen’s witnesses whether his claim, or rather the one submitted by the latter’s sister – interest for eight years, l. 17-19, which would mean more than doubling the original debt! – is true. And, if indeed so (as we must supply before l. 31), he can do with him what he wants.

5, 8, 17. The scribe writes the name Aššur-šamšī always without the divine determinative before UTU;  his god Aššur was “his sun”, but was not equated with the sungod.
10. The purpose of the action designated as karābum (see the note one l. 11) is indicated as ana li-ta-tim, which is difficult. There is a noun litum (lit(t)um or lītum) in OA, four occurrences of which CAD L 151 registers under lē/ītu, “cheek, side”, assuming (under 2,c, on the basis of occurrences in OB letters of the qualification ša litika) a meaning “persons and assets for which one is responsible / under one’s authority”, stating for OA: “with the connotation of assets”. Registering our word under lētum must have been suggested by the (unique) plene-writing in TC 2, 53:7’, “We paid 13½ shekels (of silver?) ana le-e-tí-im”, in a series of small payments for various expenses, some related to transport. But in most OA occurrences of litum, where small amounts of silver or copper are given to a person “at the time of” (inūmi or ina) his litum», and “when he made his litum” (Kt. 86/k 201:1-3, 10-12, 25-27), a meaning “assets” makes no sense. Dercksen 2011:63-65 opts for some kind of ritual, which involved a meal. He bases his interpretation on texts like Kt c/k 1147 and Kt 91/k 201. In lines 1-11 and 22-27 of the latter small amounts of silver (between 1 and 2 shekels) are given to various men, when they “went up” (elā’um) to a temple (twice that of Išhara) or “when he made his litum” (inūmi litišu ēpušu), mentioned alongside such a payment for a trip to the City (of Assur). Other texts mention the occasion as ina litišu (5 times i-li-tí-šu). But what kind of ritual or ceremonial act litum was remains unclear. And since the phrase li-tí-šu ēpuš could very well contain a plural litū (also in i-li-tí-šu), the connection with li-ta-tim in our text is also ambiguous. The meaning of the action thus remains unclear, see also Veenhof Lex., s.v. 
11-16. The meaning of ana litātim akrubka is not clear. The action apparently cost silver, since it deprived Ennam-Suen of what would accrue to him. To make up for it(?) his opponent promises to guarantee him his expenses, perhaps those due for the act of karābum. This verb is well attested in OA with a personal dative, “to pray, invoke a blessing for somebody”, usually “before” (mahar) a deity, cf. CAD K 196, 3, a. There are also a few examples of a meaning “to vow / offer” something to a deity, an “offering” (ikribum), or a precious object (RIMA 1, 43, no. 2:8; POAT 10:23). The writer of Kt 91/k 156 mentions that three times he offered / vowed small amounts (once 90 shekels of copper and twice 1 shekel of silver, probably its equivalent) for a named man (ana PN akrub). And the one of Kt 91/k 201 did the same (amounts of 1½ shekel of silver and 1 mina of copper) on three occasions, twice for a man “when he went (up) to the City” (13-18, 31-33).[7] Dercksen 2011, 64, who quotes these texts, renders the verb as “to give for a sacrifice”, but since the verb in our texts is not used with a personal dative, but with a personal accusative object (lines 11-16) this interpretation is impossible here.
