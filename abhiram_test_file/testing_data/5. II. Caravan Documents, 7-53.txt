II. CARAVAN DOCUMENTS (7-53)




II.  CARAVAN DOCUMENTS (7-53)


        As pointed out long ago by Larsen in OACP, OA trade could only function with the help of what he called “caravan documents”. He distinguished three types: a) “transport-contracts”, b) “notifying messages”, and c) “caravan accounts”. Type a) records that amounts of silver were entrusted to a transporter to bring them to Assur. Contracts concluded with transporters leading donkeys loaded with merchandise to Kanesh are very rare and Elamma’s archive contained only one of them, text *15, in an un-opened envelope. Type b) are letters written to notify the traders on both ends of what is sent off. Those written from Kanesh essentially report that amounts of silver and gold are “being carried” (the stative of našā’um) to Assur and they may state which purchases, if possible, should be made in Assur. Those written in Assur tell that the merchandise bought in Assur is “being led” (present tense of radā’um, which implies transport on donkeys) to Kanesh. Type c) “caravan accounts” also comprises letters written in Assur or in Kanesh. The former report that the silver and gold sent from Kanesh have been delivered (the past tense of wabālum in the ventive) in Assur and that goods have been bought and a caravan equipped, which is now “being lead” to Kanesh. They provide important information on quantities, prices, the equipment of the caravan, personnel engaged, export tax, expenses incurred, etc. Those written in Kanesh report on the safe arrival of the caravan there, how much taxes were paid in the local palace to clear the goods and which sales had already been effectuated. Some were sold for cash (ana itaṭlim tadānum), many via credit sales, when merchandise was given in commission to (qiāpum) or “laid upon” (nadā’um ina ṣēr) traveling agents, and that some  were still in store. Records that list the expenses incurred during the trip from Assur to Anatolia, which probably served to settle accounts with the harnessers who had received “loose tin” for meeting them, also be long to the caravan documents. But it is difficult to connect them with a caravan journey documented in the above mentioned texts, because they do not mention names. They have been treated separately in Ch. VII, where the geographical data they provide are summarily analyzed. 
        Elamma’s archive contains a number of these informative texts, which show that he was a trader of the “classical” type, like Pūšu-kēn and Imdīlum, for whom imports of textiles and tin from Assur were the basis of his business. These texts show the size of his trade, but only in general terms, since all these documents are undated and we do not know how many years they cover. The texts are also important in revealing who were Elamma’s main business associates: his “representatives” in Assur, who received the money arriving and carried out purchases for him, the men who transported the goods, who were the traders with whom E. cooperated in shipping silver and gold to Assur, and who were his friends and relatives in Assur. 
        There are interrelations between these various categories of records, because a shipment of silver sent to Assur and registered in a transport contract, may also be the subject of a “notifying letter”, reporting on its arrival and use. There are a few examples of this in the letters presented below, but in what follows these texts are presented in groups according to the classification proposed by Larsen; correlations are pointed out in the notes and comments accompanying the texts. We also have a number of letters written by and especially to Elamma (*19 and *20 written by Elamma, *21-*26 addressed to him by various persons), which provide information on the arrival and use of shipments of silver and merchandise, whose content makes them somewhat similar to notifying messages, but they apparently deal with transactions inside Anatolia. I have added them as a separate category, B.1. “Letters sent inside Anatolia”,  at the end of the section devoted to “notifying messages”. To category B.2. “Letters sent from Assur” I have added the short letters *31-*32, addressed to Elamma by a certain Iddin-Aššur, to whom merchandise was entrusted. They provide information common to “notifying letters” sent from Assur, but were probably sent when the transporter was already en route. A second addition is the memo *33, also dealing with a shipment of merchandise belonging to his brother, probably drawn up by Elamma in Kanesh rather than in Assur.


A. Transport contracts (7-15)


1. Shipment of silver and gold to Assur


The term “transport contract” is problematic, since the texts are not contracts made with the transporter to whom the silver and gold were entrusted, who would normally have sealed the contract, which should have stated his obligation to deliver the goods in Assur (šēbulum). One may compare Old Babylonian contracts about such “consignments”, šē/ūbultum, to which even the Code of Hammurabi and a later royal decree[1] pays attention. Most texts state that a named transporter “has brought the silver (etc.) away”, for which the past tense ūbil is used, which does not include the notion of “to deliver” at its destination, for which the ventive ublam, “he brought here”, is used in letters from Assur that report on the safe arrival of the silver. One may compare *39:37, a “caravan account” sent from Assur, which does not write that the transporter “is leading it to you’ (iradde’akkum), but uses the past tense, irde’akkum, apparently because he has already departed. Some contracts specify the goal of the shipment, “to the City” or “to my investors/representatives”, some also its purpose, e.g. to buy tin and/or textiles (*9:5-9; 14: 8; cf. “to (buy and) stock barley”, 13:5-7). A few also mention that for that purpose the silver etc. was entrusted (paqādum) to the transporter, which is the only verb used in *11. The end of the texts mention the witnesses in whose presence this had happened, but we have not a single “transport contract” from Kanesh preserved in a sealed envelope, only several memorandums with the texts of two or more such ‘contracts” (e.g. *10). Most texts also mention that the excise (nishatum), to be levied in Assur, had been added to the silver shipped and that the transporter had been paid his fee (šaddu’assu šabbû).[2]

10. Kt 91/k 353 (1-210-91)


This large memorandum (8.2 x 5.5 cm) lists, with verbal forms in the first person singular, that in all almost 80 minas of silver were entrusted to various transporters for shipment to Assur. It must be a compilation of a number of separate transport contracts, for lines 8-12 are identical to the contract *11. Although transport contracts are not dated, it seems likely that these shipments took place during a restricted period, perhaps of one year. The shipments of twice 30 and once 15 minas were entrusted to Ennam-Aššur, Adad-pilah and Aššur-rē’ī respectively, and the latter also took along a series of smaller packets for various persons (l. 50). That among the women mentioned as recipients of silver in the final part (lines 38ff.), Elamma’s daughter Ummī-Išhara figures and that some of these amounts sent were supplied by Lamassutum (lines 39, 46) indicates that the person speaking is Elamma. Some payments of silver are also meant for his associates and representatives in Assur, Dadiya, Ennam-Aššur, Aššur-rēṣī, who receive the yield of textiles they had sent to Elamma and which had sold for them (lines 29-33). These cases are exceptional in mentioning the origin of the silver (cf. *9:11-15).

B. Notifying messages (16-33)


Notifying messages sent by Elamma from Kanesh to Assur are addressed to his investors (ummiānū), his representatives, especially Dadiya, Ennam-Aššur and Aššur-rēṣī, and to his business associate Hinnāya and his brother Ali-ahum. We also meet the representatives as writers of notifying messages and caravan accounts sent from Assur to Kanesh,  A few other notifying messages that also report on the shipment and arrival of silver, were sent inside Anatolia: *19 by Elamma and Aššur-dān, *20 by Elamma to La-qēp, and *21 by Hinnāya to Elamma and Aššur-šulūlī. A few of them seem to have been sent from somewhere in Anatolia to Elamma in Kanesh

3. Letters sent from Assur


Most of the next letters were sent to Elamma by his business associates or respresentatives in Assur, Aššur-rēṣī, Ennam-Aššur and Dadiya, to whom Elamma sent his notifying messages from Kanesh. Among their writers we also meet his brother Ali-ahum, Elamma’s business associates Hinnāya and Iliya, and there is one letter (*30) which Elamma must have sent to Kanesh when he was in Assur.

Notes
The restoration of the missing or damaged figures (which take into account rem,aining traces and available room) is based on the fact that there were 15 2/3 minas of silver available (lines 5-6) and after the expenses of lines 8-15 enough remained to buy textiles for which 4 donkeys (this figure is required by their price of 75 shekels) were needed, which required harness, l. 25, which would have required ca. 10-12 shekels (see OACP 150f.). The figure of l. 18 is demanded by l. 19 and the figure for the “additions” in line 10 fits the space available, while a percentage of ca. 2,3% is acceptable (the same as the rate mentioned in *40:19-21). Assuming that the words [...]16 TÚG.HI.A, preserved on a tiny fragment, belong to line 16, the damaged number of textiles bought probably was 96 (it begins with a Winkelhaken, hence less than 100), which would mean 24 pieces per donkey and at 5 shekels apiece they would have cost 8 minas of silver. 
15. The “girl” probably was Elamma’s daughter Ummī-Išhara, an ugbabtu-priestess in Assur. 
26-27. The extra amount probably was the result of a (final) settlement of the accounts of the caravan, as described in OAI 175f. 
31. The “harnessers” frequently used part of their “working capital” (1/2 mina of silver in l. 23) to buy textiles in Assur, which they could sell with a profit in Anatolia.
Comments
The letter gives a good picture of what happened in Assur and how a trader acquired the silver needed to make purchases there. A large amount of silver derived from Elamma’s participation in a caravan (ellutum), described in OAI 168 as “groups formed on an ad hoc basis for the joint transport of merchandise in a caravan. The group consists of a ‘leader’ and of individuals known to him”. In our case the leader was Elamma’s brother, but Elamma also participated in caravans led by various other persons, as is clear from *5. That the silver available was what “remained” (l. 6) from a much bigger amount, could mean that for equipping an earlier caravan silver was borrowed (as was the case in *27:5f. and *41:5f.), which had to be repaid. A second source was Elamma’s investment in his brother’s joint-stock capital (also mentioned in *4), which here yielded 5 1/3 minas of silver, called “your dividends” (šipkātum), paid out by its manager, the tractator (see Larsen 1977:137); the can be said to “acrrue to” (kašādum) somebody.

5-8. At the normal rate of interest the debt would have been contracted more than three months ago..
11-12. Išertum, usually in the plural, is probably derived from išarum, “correct, right”, cf. AHw 394b, “normale Menge”(?), accepted by Larsen 1967, 118. CAD E and I/J do not know the word, but CAD M/1, 429, s.v. maṭû, 1, 2’, translates 5 GÍN ina išerātim imṭi in TC 2, 14:23 by ‘5 shekels were missing from the “normal amounts”. This translation takes ina as partitive, but ina could also be instrumental, which would make išerātum refer to a specific procedure or treatment of the metal, for which I suggested long ago (AOATT note 88) the normalizing and finishing of the cast silver, which would entail some loss of weight. A third possibility is to take maṭā’um ina as “being less than, fall short of”, just like its opposite, watārum ina, can mean “be in excess of, more than”.[4] “Being less than the normal amounts” could mean that the weight of the purified silver fell short of what it should be or of the weight mentioned in the accompanying letter. The opposite is the case in AKT 6, 4:13f., where a shipment of tin transported to Anatolia “proved to be 2 1/3 minas in excess of the iširātum of the City Hall“. This suggests that the exact weight of the tin was assessed in the City Hall by means of its weights and Dercksen, OAI 17, even suggests that the tin was melted there and cast into ingots of standard size and weight, which might be the meaning of išerātum. Nevertheless, I have my doubts about a meaning “normal weight/quantity”, since loss of weight ina išerātim is similar to and mentioned alongside losses ina ṣa’’udim, “by melting” and ina masā’im, by washing”, where ina + infinitive is certainly instrumental. See now my analysis of the terminology and procedures in Veenhof 2014a.
12-13. A second reason for loss of weight is kabāsum ana massuhūtim. Kabāsum means “to reduce, to deduct”, not as the physical result of a metallurgical treatment, but as an administrative measure: the loss of weight is accepted and written off in the accounts. The abstract noun massuhuttum,  “dirtiness, bad quality”, is derived from the adjective massuhum, frequently used of metals, cf. Kt n/k 492:25-29 (courtesy of Günbattı): “We checked the silver under your seal and ....... we deducted 2 shekels each (mina?) for/as refining” (l. 29, 2 GÍN.TA ana ṣa-ra-pu-tim nikbus). In both cases ana + abstract does not refer to a particular treatment, but to its effects, which can be quantified, “loss of volume as (result of the) refining”. In CCT 3, 41a:3f. ṣarrupūtum is quantified at 1 mina 6 shekels of silver. See also Veenhof 2014a.
14-19. I assume that the quote from the letter the writers had received ended with line 16 and that the next lines report on Elamma’s reaction. But how could the 1 mina he paid be used to pay the debt in Assur if Elamma was Anatolia? Did he give them permission to take it from his assets in Assur?
28. I take É nērubma as short for ana bēt tamkārim nērubma kaspam ana ṣibtim nilqe; see for examples Veenhof 1999: 66f. and *27:5f.
29. The –um added to GÍN is common as locative ending, to express the rate of interest, “per shekel”, but here, with the amount of silver as subject of ittuar, it should be the nominative ending, which is strange since it is added to 15 GÍN. 
32-34. A pair of week eponyms attested during the same month in eponymy year REL 91, in ICK 1, 104:8  and ICK 2, 11A:4 // B:8, but there is no room to insert the name of the epoynym in line 34. Larsen observes that it remarkable to find a loan contracted in Assur dated by hamuštu-eponyms, which are a typical “colonial feature”; it suggests that the people in Assur were informed about the succession of week eponyms.
41-43. The end of the letter phrases the well-known concern to prevent the interest from increasing and to get the original debt-note back. 
Comment
Of the original debt  of 51 minas of silver, plus its accumulated interests (plural) - 4 minas 15 shekels (at the normal rate of interest this means a period of ca. 3 1/3 months) - after the payment of the 46 minas delivered by Abu-šalim and the collection of some outstanding debts (apparently owed to the addressees; lines 19-24) 4 1/4 minas remained (l. 27). With the interest due (apparently for 9 months) this has now become 5 minas 10 ½ shekels. The mention of interest, together with the wish to obtain the release of their debt-note (line 43), implies that this amount was again borrowed, presumably from the same money-lender, and that he drew up a dated, new debt-note for this amount, after which the debtors would have got back their original debt-note for 51 minas of silver. My interpretation of the damaged lines 34-40 is tentative. The writers seem to say that for the time being the original debt-note remains in their possession. But when they receive the amount due to them – the new loan plus its interest – it will be cancelled (by them; limūt). What they add in l. 40 is lost.


2. Accounts and memorandums about caravans inside Anatolia


I present here a few letters, dealing with the arrival of a caravan in Kanesh, addressed to Elamma, apparently when he was staying elsewhere in Anatolia and had to be informed on what happened in Kanesh. He must have taken them home when returning to Kanesh, where they ended up in his archives. I add some memorandums that use the first personal singular and of which he could well be the writer, although this is not completely certain. But the fact that*46: 11-13 his wife Lamassutum is said to have taken some of the kutānu-textiles that had been cleared in the palace supports this interpretation. Moreover, the trader Iliya, the arrival of whose caravan is reported in *43 (see line 6), appears as the first writer of the caravan account *39, sent from Assur to Elamma.

XI). That for the import-tax fits the norm (2 shekels per šuqlum), but the amount of šaddu’utu-tax, usually paid to the Assyrian authorities, is unbelievably high (ca. 17%) and might cover older obligations. The renewed payment to Ku’uku of additional ‘loose tin’ is puzzling; was it for transport inside Anatolia?

A balance payment by the palace (“they”) of 1 shekel of silver alongside an import-tax of 2½ textiles is odd in two ways. D. Joint-caravans (ellutum) and “declared value” (awītum) (49-53)


        The first two records presented below deal with a trader’s awītum, that is the “declared value” of a caravan or the shares of the traders participating in a joint caravan (ellutum). It is a system whereby the value of its load is expressed in tin as single valuta, whereby one textile was valued at 2 minas and one donkey at between 1 1/3 and 2 minas. It served as a basis for sharing and apportioning expenses and losses incurred, especially the road-tax (da’atum), and it may also have been used en route to be shown to authorities levying taxes. See for a discussion ana anlysis of the system OAI Ch. 8. Some new “awītum lists” were published by C. Günbattı, ArAn 5 (2002), 81ff. 
        The joint caravans, called ellutum, are named after one or two traders who lead and organized it. There are many records, such as the ones presented below, that deal with the revenues of its participants (in which the verb napālum is used) and mention what they obtain, usually in various stages, whereby the texts distinguish what comes from the “holding”, (mišittum), presumably a fund stocked in the colonial office, and what accrues to them as share in the profit, called “the one third parts” (šalšātum). See the discussion and analysis in OAI Ch. 9, who could already use the texts published below.

Comment on texts 49-53
The preceding texts belong to the category of short notes that record the claims individual traders have (on the basis of their participation or investment, expressed by the verb adāmu, always in the stative) on a share in the fund of a collective caravan (ellutum), presumably stocked and administered in the colonial office, discussed in OAI 169-170 and 175-176 (on the settlement of accounts). He takes mišittum, “what is deposited”, “holding”, as a term for a storeroom, the goods kept there and the value they represent. Our records fit the pattern whereby ca. two-thirds of the claim is on the “holding”  (note Kt 86/k 101:14-18, šinepē ina / mišit ellitim ....ilaqqe), and one-third on “the one-third fund”, but in *53 the latter is only half the amount of the share in the “holding”, which is also the case Kt 94/k 545 (courtesy Barjamovic), where the writer will take 5 ½ minas of silver from the mišittum of the caravan and 2⅔ minas as šalšātum.
The mention in *51 of a balancing payment for textiles together with statements about collecting silver from the “holding” (mišittum) and the “one-third fund” (šalšātum) occurs in several other texts. There are differences in the formulation and the question is whether they are only terminological (use of synonyms, short and abbreviated versions) or refer to different procedures. Full versions can reveal what is going on and for our text Kt a/k 868, (presented in OAI 176) is  illuminating. It deals with the accounting of a trader’s share in the caravan (ellutum) lead by Š. and it is helpfully explicit and gives exact figures. It starts by mentioning that the trader had “deposited” 25⅔textiles in the colonial office, apparently his share in a large lot (hence the fraction of a textile) shipped by the caravan and sold to the palace that paid for it via the colony  (or perhaps, less likely, the lot figured in an enterprise organized by the colony itself). The trader will receive for his textiles 12 shekels of silver apiece and three entries specify how the payment will take place: 1) silver to be received from the “one-third fund” of the caravan; 2) silver he has as his share in the “holding of the caravan”; and 3) silver that will be balanced (napālum) by leader of the caravan, This agrees with *51, whose figures for the payments from the “holding” and the “one-third fund” imply a price of ca. 10 shekels per textile, which could rise to 12 shekels if there was also (though not mentioned here) a balancing payment by the leader of the caravan. Similar accounts show that payments by the palace for textiles bought were registered in and probably channeled through the colonial office, so that we read about “the holding of the caravan in the palace” (mišit ellitim ina ekallim), which presumably is an abbreviation for what is explicitly formulated in  Kt a/k 814:30-33, “I have a share (admāku) of x silver in the caravan of PN in the palace; I have been registered (for it) on the tablet of the colony” (other texts also write “I have been registered (for it) in the palace”).
        Depending the sitation the words “they will balance me” (*51: 2) apparently can refer to the caravan or have the colony as subject, e.g. in Kt c/k 331 (courtesy Dercksen): “18 textiles the colonial office will balance me”.  Further research, which cannot be undertaken here, is needed to distinguish the various modalities and procedures (cf. OAI 176, note 475).

2. Small amounts of silver and gold sent to Assur
Many smaller amounts to be delivered to particular persons are specified by their weight or called riksum (= ri), “bundle, small packet”, šēbultum (= šeb), “consignment, gift” (esp. for women), or ikribū, “votive gift” or silver derived from temple funds. This silver often is the yield of textiles they had made and sent off, entrusting them to trader as “free loan” (tadmiqtum) a term that also denotes their yield (cf. AOATT 111). The silver sent can be designated simply as ša fPN, but also as šēbultum ša or šēbultaša ša fPN (with a double genitive). In many cases this seems to mean “shipment for fPN”, but it can also designate its owner, who sends it to Assur, which must be the case if the women (persons)  mentioned are known to have lived in Kanesh.

3. Merchandise bought for or shipped to Elamma in Assur
Notifying messages and caravan accounts list tin (in talents and minas; sealed and loose together), various textiles (te; kutānum = ku; šūrum = šu, a dark textile, also used for wrapping the tin; li = liwītum, textile for wrapping), including those “for wrapping” (the tin) and those “of the harnessers” (kaṣṣārū). Transporter or “harnesser” is the man who is subject of the verbal forms ublam, naš’akkum, or iraddeakkum  (rare: irdeakkum). A few times we find instructions to buy tin or textiles; in *9:1-5 to buy tin, in *9:6-9 to buy kutānū, in *18:8-12 kutānū if tin is too expensive. Some reports only mention what merchandise is on its way or has just arrived, without stating how much silver was spent in Assur to buy it and for equipping the caravan. I have reconstructed the amounts of silver spent on the basis of standard or average prizes, 15 shekels of tin for 1 shekel of silver and 5 shekels of silver for the purchase of 1 kutānu-textile. These averages yield reliable results, but some textiles are somewhat cheaper or more expensive (e.g. “Akkadian ones”, in *42). The prices for the tin fluctuated considerably (see Veenhof 1988, 250ff.) and there is also some difference between the (generally slightly cheaper and much) smaller quantities of annak qātim, “tin for in the hand” (not sealed, to be used for expenses; šq in what follows) and the sealed tin shipped in packets (šuqlum). We find tin at: 12 ½:1 in *35:19, 13:1 in *38:20 (both šq), 13:1 in *34:22, 13 2/3:1 in *28:12 (called “expensive”),  14 ¼:1 in *40:8, 14 1/3:1 in *36:8, 15:1 in *18:8 (acceptable price) and *39:19 (šq), 15 5/12:1 in *39:16, and 17¼:1 in *37:19. A variation of up to 30% is substantial high prices could induce traders to buy textiles instead of tin (see below note a on *18 and *82:7-13). We are not informed about the reasons for these fluctuations and also cannot date them.

4.  Data on “harnessers” (kaṣṣārum) ad other transporters
Insight in the status of transporters, apart from the names listed in the tables of § 1 and 3, is supplied by refereces to the “working capital” (be’ūlātum = be) supplied by contract to “harnessers” (kaṣṣārum = ka), who thereby enter the service of the creditor/employer, by payments to other transporters not qualified as such (in *19 also designated as “rādi luqūtim of PN”), and by references to small numbers of textiles belonging to them that are taken along by the caravan (general statements that a shipment of textiles includes an unspecified number of “textiles of the harnessers” are not listed). Ka behind a name means that he is identified as kaṣṣārum, (ka) that he must be one, since his “working capital (be) is mentioned.  Amounts of 25 to 30 shekels of silver given to transporters (also when these amounts are simply listed as a man’s debt in memoranda) in many cases probably are to be interpreted as “working capital”, also when the term be’ūlātum itself is not used.

There is an overlap with the names in §§ 1 and 3, but several men, attested as transporters, are not identifiable as kaṣṣārū.[6] Some may have worked under an existing contract, so that the (earlier) payment of a working capital is not mentioned. Others, such as Aššur-nādā, the son of Aššur-idī, in *38:6-7, and Dān-Aššsur, the son of Šalim-ahum, in *38:4-5, both delivering amounts of silver, are young traders, who in other texts too ship goods and lead caravans, not on the basis of a service contract that bound them to a particular employer, but by accepting “transport commissions” from several traders. Others may have had a similar status, but that is difficult to prove. Aššur-nādā, Balṭu-šar and Hanānum, listed in memorandums (*72:1f, *69:36f. and *70:32) as owing 30 shekels of silver to Elamma, may well have received that silver as “working capital” (as we know from other sources of Aššur-rē’ī and Šumi-abiya, who are listed in the same way) and accordingly have been harnessers.
