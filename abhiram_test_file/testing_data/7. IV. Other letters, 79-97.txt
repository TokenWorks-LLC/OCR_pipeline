IV. OTHER LETTERS (79-97)


A. Letters of Elamma (79-82)


The letters written by Elamma and, apart from 80*, sent off to Assur, must be archive copies kept by him. Letter 82* is written by Elamma and his business partner Kurub-Ištar, who also figures as his co-addressee in 28*, 31* and *94 (cf. 8*:28). Letter 105* appears in Ch. V, in a section dealing with “confirmation” (ka’’unum).
 
79. Kt 91/k 317 (1-174-91)


A letter addressed by Elamma to four men in Assur, the first three probably his representatives, about copper still owed to him there. The two men who owe him copper figure among the addressees, which means that they themselves could read or hear Elamma’s request. This is not surprising, because they belonged to Elamma’s business partners in Assur, who figure in many letters exchanged between Assur and Kanesh. One of the debtors, Atāya, who occurs more often as a business associate of Elamma is addressed again in lines 17ff. (note “please my brother”, in l. 24) on a debt owed by his father.

Comment
Elamma has “taken” (in consignment?) a shipment of tin and textiles belonging to six business associates in Assur on the basis of an agreement, which probably meant that they had drawn up a sealed contract mentioning how much silver he would pay for it and when. It must also have contained the clause quoted in lines 11-15a, that specified how Elamma could indemnify himself should the colony levy šaddu’utu-tax on the shipment. This tax, recently discussed in OAI 110ff. and AKT 5, 85ff., here must be the high one of 3/60 (or 5%) of the value of a shipment, called “the šaddu’utum of the Kanesh colony”. The 36 minas of tin taken as tax (l. 29) then imply a shipment with a declared value of 12 talents of tin and it might have consisted of two donkey-loads of tin and three of textiles. This tax was a “once only payment” from which the important “licensed” traders, the so-called “dātum-payers”, were exempt and this must have been the reason why Elamma, asserting that the merchandise belonged to him, protested against its levy. The mere fact that the contract included a clause about a possible attempt of the colony to levy this tax, indicates that Elamma was not certain whether switching the ownership of the shipment would work. And indeed, the colony rejected Elamma’s request, because it considered the merchandise the property of Iliya, since it had been combined with, added to his assets, perhaps in storage or when it entered the palace. That such things happened is clear from CCT 3, 28b:14-16, where in view of the taxation by the palace, “the textiles were combined with those of A.” (ṣubātū ana ša A. šaknū). Iliya (see p. XXXVI) was a business associate of Elamma in Assur, as shown by *102:5, where a trader addressing Elamma, speaks of “Iliya of yours” (plur., kunu’um), i.e. of your firm. He was also the first-mentioned investor in Elamma’s joint-stock fund (*2:1), Elamma corresponds with him (*3:1) and sends him silver (*12:5). Iliya’s son Lā-qēp apparently lived in Anatolia and cooperated with Elamma. 
        The refusal of the colony to consider Elamma as the owner of the shipment meant that the Assyrian authorities en route in Anatolia made him pay a šaddu’utu-tax of 36 minas of tin. Because 6 minas of it were somehow for Kūn-ilum’s account, Elamma in lines 33-39 asks the addressees to send him these 30 minas from Assur without delay, because (lines 36-37) “I do not (wish to) take it here from your silver”, a statement which shows his good will, because the agreement quoted in lines 11-15 authorized him to do (lines 11-15; was Iliya the manager of the joint operation?). A problem is that these 30 minas of tin in lines 33-35 are described as being the (total of) the dātum, muṭā’ū and the nishatum incurred by the shipment. Of these deductions (the verb used is normally ṣahhurum) nishatum is the tax paid to the local palace on imported tin, dātum the “road-tax” paid during the caravan trip from Assur to Anatolia, and muṭā’ū the underweight established when upon arrival a sealed packet of tin proved to contain less than normal (usually 65 minas) or than was stated in a relevant caravan report or bill of loading. But such expenses seem to be quite different from the šaddu’utu-tax paid to the colonial office, unless the latter was somehow an addition of such costs or expenses, not paid en route but charged by the colony. This raises a series of questions about the nature of these expenses and the role of the colonial office, which require a much broader analysis than is possible here 
        I only mention here the possibility that the “road-tax” paid was the one for the final stage of the journey, from Hahhum to Kanesh, due to the Kanesh colony (cf. OAI 159 and see for many references to road-tax paid for this stage, Geography 89f.). This happened in Kt 94/k 351 (courtesy Barjamovic), where for a shipment of 140 minas of tin (one donkey-load) the transporter received 2 minas 17½ shekels (ca. 1,6% of the value of the load) as compensation for what he had paid as road-tax from Hahhum (until Kanesh)”. By also mentioning an underweight of 2 minas and the payment of 4 minas as nishatum,[1] this text is a nice parallel to our letter and it may support the idea that the term šaddu’utu-tax in our text was in fact the addition of several expenses paid to or charged by the colony. Another possibility is that the costs were those of transport further into Anatolia, beyond Kanesh. That the goods were indeed shipped on from Kanesh is shown by Elamma’s words “wherever they collected the šaddu’utu-tax for the colony” (l. 26f.), which must have happened inside Anatolia, at other colonies, as shown in OAI 112ff. and my observations in AKT 5:85-89. That Elamma had appealed “here” (in Kanesh) is understandable, because this tax was levied for the Kanesh colony and traders protesting against it in another colony had to go there to try to obtain a refund (AKT 5, 3:16-21). That the import-tax was levied upon arrival in an Anatolian town and the “underweight” was established when the packets were opened there, needs no proof. And we also have evidence for the levying of nishatum en route in Anatolia, in AKT 3, 34:7-9 and 15-18, twice at a bridge, at a rate of 20 and 15 shekels of copper per donkey, very small sums that looks like a “transit fee”. There are also a few occurrences of the payment of “road-tax” (dātum) in connection with transport inside Anatolia, e.g. in AKT 3, 34:1-6, where expenses for a trip from Wahšušana to Šaladuwar were “115 shekels of copper each” (donkey?) for the road-tax, donkey fodder and an inn, and in Kt c/k 263:39-41 (courtesy Dercksen), which mentions that for a shipment of copper “from Kanesh until Wahšušana the road-tax amounted for me to 3 minas per (donkey?)”. In Kt 87/k 465 (courtesy Hecker) amounts of copper paid as road-tax for trips from Šaladuwar to Burušhattum, vice versa, and from Wahšušana to Šaladuwar are mentioned, and in Kt 94/k 403 one for a trip from Hahhum to Burušhattum.

B. Letters to Elamma, alone or with others (83-97)


The letters are presented on the basis the alphabetical order of the names of their writers. Most writers are represented by one letter, but Ahu-waqar and Aššur-rē’ī by two, and most letters are addressed to Elamma alone, but *91 to Hinnāya and Elamma. In *92, to Elamma and Ali-ahum, and *94, to Elamma and Kurub-Ištar, the second addressee was the main one. In these letters Elamma is not mentioned in the body of text, nor asked to do something, but the writers apparently wished to keep him informed of what they wrote. This is understandable because the addressees and writers were his relatives (his brother Ali-ahum in *92,  his son-in-law Kūn-ilum in *94) and business associates (Hinnāya and Kurub-Ištar in *91 and *94).

Comment to *83-84.
In both letters Elamma, who must be writing from somewhere outside Kanesh, asks Ahu-waqar to find (and send him?) two different letters by Ali-ahum (presumably Elamma’s brother), one (84*:5) addressed to Elamma alone, the other (83*:6-7) to Hinnāya and Elamma. The words written on them (lapit), which are quoted, are the address on their envelopes. But since 83*:4-5 identifies the first letter as “wrapped in a rag”, the reference to “its face” (panūšu) could also mean the text on a sealed bulla attached to the packet as a label for identifying it. Various archives have indeed yielded sealed bullae with such inscriptions (see Özgüç-Tunca 2001, 322ff., Kt c/k 832, 833, 835, 837, etc.). In *83:5-7 the statement on the inscription must refer to the envelope, for lines 9-10 mention that this letter had been sealed by two other men, apparently referring to the bulla attached to the packet (perhaps a textile wrap, as mentioned in *83:4), which contained the letter.
        In 84* it is difficult (also due to the lacuna after line 11) to decide where the quote of what Elamma wrote (starting in l. 5) ended. Are lines 7ff. part of it or the beginning of Ahu-waqar’s answer? The information in 83*:8ff., that the tablet sought was supposed to be among Aššur-ṭāb’s tablets, is interesting. It may imply that the latter, who might be Elamma’s son or  nephew,[2] had a special container with his tablets in Elamma’s house, to which Ahu-waqar had access, even during Aššur-ṭāb’s absence (l. 9). This raises the question who Ahu-waqar was, complicated by the absence of his patronym. The only patronym in our archive is in *283A:2, where, as son of Aššur-rē’ī, he figures as witness of a confrontation between Āl-ṭāb and Amur-Aššur. He might be the same man who, together with Aššuriš-tikal, writes *105 to Elamma and who, at Elamma’s instruction, according to *25:6, together with Aššur-mūtappil (presumably Elamma’s son) has to take tin belonging to Elamma.[3]
