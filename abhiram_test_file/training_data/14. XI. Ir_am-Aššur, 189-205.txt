XI.  IR’AM-AŠŠUR (189-205)                                                  






XI.  IR’AM-AŠŠUR (189-205)


        Ir’am-Aššur was the son of Id(d)i-bāni[1] and married to Šalimma, a daughter of Elamma, as shown by Kt 93/k 513:17, “Šalimma, daughter of Elamma” (courtesy C. Michel). A long letter, *206, sent by her sister Ummī-Išhara from Assur to Šalimma in Kanesh, in which she tries to mediate between her and her husband Ir’am-Aššur, who lived in Assur and whom she refused to join, is edited in Ch. XII, devoted to Šalimma .
        Ir’am-Aššur’s name is usually spelled Ir-ma-Aššur, with contraction, but occasionally Ir-am-Aššur (*226:1 // B:18) and he even occurs as Na-ra-am-Aššur, son of Id(d)i-bāni (in *233:3). He owned the seal CS 1148, which carries an inscription mentioning its previous owner, a man from Ebla (see the drawing in Teissier 1994, 233 no. 527), used three times on texts from the archive. This chapter presents the texts of or concerning him that ended up in Elamma’s archive, no doubt in consequence of his marriage. But he is also known from his own archive, found in the first house excavated in 1994, texts numbered Kt 94/k 117-184, provisional transcriptions of which were kindly made available to me by Cécile Michel.[2]        
        Ir’am-Aššur was a trader active in Anatolia and who traveled between Kanesh and Assur, where he also stayed for some time. He occurs dozens of times in texts from different archives,[3] with dates ranging from eponymy year REL 92 (*195) to year 111 (Kt 94/k 143). He apparently had some form of cooperation with Elamma’s sons, in particular after Elamma’s death. They considered him their senior (calling him “father and boss”) and in their trading operations also handled merchandise belonging to him. That he was somewhat older is confirmed by the appearance of his son, named after his grandfather, as witness for him in the undated *192:15-16. From his own archive we have a record relating to the division between him and his brother Aššur-rabi of the inheritance of their father, witnessed by four men (Kt 94/k 157, courtesy of C. Michel). It states “Ir’am-Aššur and Aššur-rabi, sons of Id(d)i-bāni, have sworn an oath by the City and they have been entitled[4], so that they will not raise claims against each other for anything, whatsoever, belonging to their paternal estate. (11) What their father’s sister and their own sister will leave behind (warkutum) will belong to the two of them” (ša kilišunuma). Here I focus on what texts from Elamma’s archive tell us; his business has to be reconstructed when his archive is published.


A.  Ir’am-Aššur and Ennam-Aššur (189-194)


The next six texts document business relations between Ennam-Aššur, the eldest son of Elamma (henceforth E.) and his clearly older brother-in-law Ir’am-Aššur (henceforth I.), whom he calls “my father, my boss”. The first three are letters by E. to I., the next two are contracts by which amounts of silver of E. are entrusted (paqādum) for transport to Assur by I. The last text is a letter sent to I.  by his  representatives dealing with textiles handled by E.

Interpretation
These six documents document the rather difficult relations between Elamma’s son Ennam-Aššur (E.) and his senior brother-in-law Ir’am-Aššur (I.), who was married to E.’s sister Šalimma (cf. Ch. XII). E. is engaged in the trade in tin and textiles and also handles merchandise belonging to I. (190*:21, “my textiles”, which he entrusts to him, *191:31-32) and he seems to be in financial problems, because he has to pay one or more creditors in Assur. The background could be the death of his father (who is not mentioned; his mother Lamassutum features in *193:5) and the ensuing liquidation of his business, which must have entailed the payment of his debts by his sons. In this his sister in Assur, Ummī-Išhara, must have played a role and this could be the reason why she “needed” (hašāhum) silver of E. sent to Assur (*189:44-45, *190:13, *191:12-13). E. also encountered problems in Anatolia, because he had been stuck in Hattum for eight months, presumably due to political troubles. He utters various complaints against I., because the latter did not pay him his travel expenses and did not live up to his promises of providing him with tin from Assur. The documents illustrate the problems a junior trader might run into and his dependence on the cooperation with a senior partner. The differences between the texts show that the same events could be portrayed in different perspective, partly also in different order, which may reflect the mood of the rather desperate E. who, not without some rhetorical skill, mixes complaints with appeals for help and a plea for sticking to promises. The letters are also interesting in showing the commercial realities and problems that are at the basis of seemingly simple transport contracts and consignments. The following sketch of the events divides them into various episodes. 
a) Mentioned first in *190:4-14//*191:3-14 and later in *189:38-45: Some time ago (“pre-viously” in *190 and *191) E. had sent 2 minas 57 shekels of silver with (išti) Šu-Suen, son of Iliya, from Hattum to I. in Kanesh (*191:5), asking him to take it along to Assur. This is also recorded in the undated contract *193, whereby Šu-Suen hands this silver over to E.’s mother and I. in order to have the latter bring it to Assur in order “to pay it for the debt E. owes to tamkārum”, his creditor or boss. In the letters (*189:40-41 // *190:7-8) however, E. states that I. was to use it to buy tin for him, “some 10 minas” (*190:8), to be sent to him in Anatolia (*189:41, ušellâm, *190:8, ušebbalam). There it could be sold by E. with profit and the silver earned in this way (*189:42//*190:10, kaspum elliam) would be used to pay his creditor/boss (tamkārum), “on the basis of my tablet”; *189:42-3 writes “I will pay silver to my tamkārum and (to do so?) send it” (to Assur); *190:10-12 is shorter, “I will send the silver to my tamkārum”. Was this the original intention or a later adaptation of the goal (by inserting an intermediary step), stipulated in contract *193?  In Assur, however  (*189:44-45 // *190:13), as I. had told him, E.’s sister, no doubt the priestess Ummī-Išhara, required the silver and I. had given it to her, ana ša ṭuppika (*189:45//*190:14//*191:14). This suggests that there was a contractual basis for it, possibly a recorded liability or promise of E., perhaps the basis for the stipulation in text *193 that I. would use the silver to pay E.’s debts. I assume that Ummī-Išhara’s “need” for the silver was due to the fact that, after their father’s death, she was engaged in liquidating his firm, which implied paying the commercial debts that were the liability of the male heirs, her brothers. She accordingly must have obliged I. to give her the silver E. had earned and not use it for a new commercial enterprise. The same feature can be observed after Pūšu-kēn’s death, when his daughter in Assur, Ahaha, is desperately trying to pay the family’s debts, while her brothers in Anatolia only think of trading.
b) Having disappointed E. by the outcome of a) I., “later” (urkītam *189:46//190:15), when he was about to leave (for Assur?), came up with a new plan to help E. and “give him courage” (*189:46-49//190:15-20). E. has to give him all the silver he has available and I. will buy for him 10 minas of tin, which will allow E. to earn money. E. manages to collect the modest sum of 1 mina 44 shekels of silver (*189:50-52//190:36-39//191:22-25), sufficient to buy tin in Assur (normally at a rate of ca. 15 shekels of tin for 1 shekel of silver), which he “gave to I. for making purchases” (*189:54) in Hurama, as witnessed by A. and I. (*190:39-40//191:26-27), “under my seals” (*190:39//191:25). This transaction is recorded in *192, witnessed by the same two men, but here (lines 7-8) the silver is said to be “under the seals of tamkārum”, which means of its anonymous owner, perhaps to prevent other creditors of E. to seize it. Since the transaction is for the benefit of E. he has to pay for the costs of the transport, both the “excise” and the “transport fee”, as *189:50-51//*190:37 and *192:2-6 duly mention. *189:54 then continues with E.’s complaint that in exchange for this silver I. has thus far not sent him any tin and in lines 56-57 asks to do so now; cf. *190:43-44, where E. also asks what he has done wrong (šillatam rašā’um) to earn this treatment. 
c) In *190: 21-34 this sad story is preceded by a report about problems with the sale of a lot of textiles (*190:21, “my textiles”), which I. had entrusted (paqādum) to E. in Luhusaddiya (*191:31-32). A situation with unsafe roads (stative of sahā’um, D-stem) and unyielding local palaces (dannum, *189:16) had obliged E. to wait many months “in the interior” until the trade could be resumed, 8 months in *189:17, 7 months in *190:26 and 6 months in *191:34! The delay had also caused damage of the textiles by moths, apparently because they had not been regularly aired. When they had been finally “salvaged” (šalāhum) their sale proved difficult and their yield was poor, on average only 15 shekels of silver apiece, in all 2½ minas (the 3½ minas of *190:32 may be a mistake). This silver had been received (?) and sealed by I.’s representatives, but had not yet been shipped off (*189:29-32//*190:32:34, ša kīma kuāti, plural // šazzuzātikasic, cf. šazzuzātika in l. 30). Letter *190:32-37 reveals that this was because I.’s plan to let E.’s sister, in this case to all appearances I.’s wife Šalimma (who stayed in Kanesh), take it along on her intended journey to Assur, had failed, because she could not travel due to severe cold in Central Anatolia.
        This story is confirmed by letter *194, written by Dān-Aššur and Ennam-Suen, who must be I.’s “representatives” mentioned in *189:31. They offer a few more details on the composition of the lot of textiles, sealed by Kulumāya (also mentioned in *190:29), which had been delivered to them by E. While *189:22 simply states that E. had brought the textiles to I.’s representatives in Wahšušana, *194:9-15 write that, because they could not be sold “here” and because they had been affected by moths, they were subsequently brought by E. to Šaladuwar, further to the west, and were sold there. Then – so we must assume - E. returned to Wahšušana, presented the silver earned to I.’s representatives, which they had him bring to I. (in Kanesh?) under their seals. This story of the textiles features also in *189:14-32, before episodes a) and b), as continuation of E.’s complaint that he still had not received compensation for his travel expenses (see below d). This is understandable, since for I. the salvage of the textiles was a condition for paying them.  
d) The issue of the travel expenses (gamrum ša harrānim) to which E. is entitled forms the beginning of *189. It was raised in Hurama, where both men met and had a discussion (lines 2-11) and the expenses were those of transporting the textiles, incurred either to get them in Hurama or (more likely) to ship them on, possibly to “Hattum”. It is not certain that this is the lot of textiles entrusted to E. in Luhusaddiya according to *191:31-32, because this town is situated north of Hurama, were the discussion about the expenses took place (*189:3). That I. had to pay expenses and wrote for E. a “document concerning the textiles” (see the note on *189:3) indicates that the textiles belonged to I. and that E. handled them for him (alongside his own business, such as the trade in tin). I. had given him only a little silver, refused to pay E.’s expenses cash (*190:7-8, ana qātim tadānum) and would pay them only after (adi, l. 9) E. had salvaged the textiles (šalāhum, *189:9, cf. šēṣu’um, ‘to get out”, l. 20), by sending him silver from Hahhum, which he failed to do. E. then reminds him of the serious problems encountered, apparently unknown to I., and tells how thanks to the pains he took (anāhum) the textiles are safe, although at the cost of expenses equal to the price of three textiles. Text *190 gives a somewhat different picture by linking episode b) with the salvaging of the textiles and by making the latter an order of I. and thus a condition for his help (*190:21-22): “You said: Exert yourself for my textiles and salvage what you can, so that they do not get lost!”  
        The geographical data of the texts have been studied in Geography 137f., 159f., with the partly duplicating footnotes 437-8 and 546-7, where Barjamovic quotes and discusses texts *189 and *191, that I had made available to him. He describes Luhuzaddiya as “located on a separate route that branched out from the main Euphrates route in Hurama” and passed Kuššara, Šamuha and Kuburnat to arrive at Durhumit, thus connecting the south with Hattum, which he defines as the eastern part of Central Anatolia, “linked to the clusters east of Kanesh” (p. 164). He then offers the following reconstruction, into which I insert between brackets some data, referring to the texts of our file: “More than 6 months beforehand, while Ennam-Aššur was in Luhuzattiya, Irma-Aššur sent him a letter (from Hurama? *189:3 – K.R.V.), some silver and an order on what to do with a consignment of textiles arriving in Hurama from Assur. Thereupon (*189:14f. //*190:23f.) Irma-Aššur continued his journey to Hahhum, where he stayed for a while (*189:10-13) before finally leaving for Assur (implied by *189:41ff.).  In the meantime Ennam-Aššur went to Hattum (also referred to as libbi mātim) (*189:18 – K.R.V.), but unrest broke out and the consignment had to be stored in villages there (*189:19). Once the situation had settled down, Ennam-Aššur sent the goods from Hattum to Wahšušana (*189:21-22, cf. *190:30). Furthermore, he sent Šu-Suen from Hattum to Irma-Aššur in Kaneš with a sum of money (*189:39, *190:5-6).” 
        A slight modification of this picture is that at the start I. himself was apparently present in Hurama, where he instructed E. about how to ship the silver earned by the textiles to Assur (*189:32f.) and where E. (on the same occasion?) gave him the 1 mina 44 shekels of silver (*190:40, *191:27, cf. *192). This asks for a different meaning for the “missive” (našpertum) I. gave to E. there (see note on *189:3). The main question, also raised by the different order in which our sources relate the events, is whether all refer to one single journey, from Hurama to Luhusaddiya, to Hattum, to Wahššušana, to Šaladuwar and perhaps eventually back to Kanesh. I find it difficult to assume that E.’s activity in Hattum, mentioned at the beginning of *190 (line 4, “previously’) was in consequence of the meeting with I. in Hurama and the acceptance of a consignment of textiles in Luhusaddiya. *191:31ff. indicate that the textiles suffered under the long delay “in the interior” of Hattum and the position of *189:15ff. also suggests that the meeting in Hurama (*190:3) preceded these events. If so, the shipment by E. of almost 3 minas of silver of his to I. in Kanesh (*190:4ff., *189:38ff.) must have preceded these events and points to an earlier stay of E. in Hattum, which he may have interrupted in order to come to Luhusaddiya / Hurama to receive a new consignment. Why this consignment was picked up in Luhusaddiya (*191:31), after a meeting in the more southern Hurama, is unclear. Geography 135 mentions that “one could travel directly from Timilkiya to Luhusaddiya and so avoid taxation in Šalahšuwa and Hurama, but this required a royal guide or smuggling”, but these are not mentioned in our text.

Comment
It is not easy to understand this record. Ir’am-Aššur apparently had received silver of Šuli, via Ennam-Suen (l. 8), for a commercial purpose, which obviously should be paid back in some way to its owner. He may have been approached by the three men mentioned in lines 1-4, among whom a son of Šuli, or he has summoned them and in front of witnesses told them that his case already had been settled by three other men, including a brother of Šuli, so that Šuli’s son (and heir) is satisfied (l. 15) and Šuli himself cannot claim anything from him. The payment apparently had been indirect, perhaps in the framework of a larger settlement of accounts, which may have taken place if indeed Šuli was not longer alive (as suggested in the comment on line 5). The three men summoned acknowledge this by the rather puzzling statement “we have satisfied Šuli with the silver”. Our understanding is also hampered by their following remark, that they leave it to the/a tamkārum to sue Ir’am-Aššur for the silver he still owes on the basis of tablet. This may also point to a more general settlement of accounts that could result in new debt-notes, at times also ones that left the creditor anonymous (calling him tamkārum), e.g. because it was not yet clear who was entitled to the money (e.g. when a firm was liquidated). But there may be other explanations, since tamkārum could also mean the manager of a joint-stock capital or the boss of a firm.

 This name is usually spelled NI-DÍ-ba-ni, but a rare spelling with initial I-, in *226:2, Ir-am-A-šur / DUMU I-dí-ba-ni, shows that NI- is to be read Ì-, but I do not know what the name means. Ir’am-Aššur's son was named after this grandfather, see *192:15-16.
[2] It contains a dozen debt-notes in which he is creditor, letters to him, records of private summonses and litigation in which he is one of the parties, a contract of the marriage of his daughter Suhkana, a slave-sale, and an agreement with his brother Aššur-rabi on the division of assets of their “paternal house”. His above-mentioned seal CS 1148 is impressed on Kt 94/k 179, which he seals as a debtor who owes 10 minas of silver to Iliya (from eponymy year REL 113).
