V. LEGAL RECORDS OF VARIOUS NATURE (98-120)


A. “Confirmation” (ka’’unum, kuta’’unum; 98-101)


        Establishing facts and recovering the truth was at times difficult due to the sophistication of the trade and because parties, witnesses and written evidence were often not in the same place. One of the strategies, when a liability was unclear or had been shifted to a third party, was to agree, at times by mediation or arbitration (as was the case in 101*), on “confirmation”. This was expressed by the verb ka’’unum, occasionally used in the G-stem, with an impersonal subject, to state that a fact had been “confirmed, proved” (see Veenhof 1995, 1729, and below 101*:10), but regularly in the D-stem, with a personal subject and a single or double object.[1] EL p. 176a ranged ka’’unum under the heading “Gestellungs-bürgschaft”, explaining it as “jemandem auf die Person/Sache ein Zugriffsrecht verschaffen”, but this is not correct, for the verb refers to proving a fact or a person’s liability.
        The D-stem occurs in various constructions (cf. CAD K 169, under kânu A, 4, b). Most common (also in Old Babylonian) is ka’’unum with a personal accusative (e.g. a presumed debtor) and with the dative of the person who had raised the claim and would benefit from the action (e.g. a creditor).  In 100*:11-14, where I. claims silver from Elamma, who asserts that A. had already received it (in the name of I.?), we read: Elamma ana I. A. uka’’an, “Elamma will confirm A.(‘s liability) for I.”. This construction is also found in TCL 1, 241, where a man denies having received a payment, adding “I will confirm for you P. and Š.” (as witnesses?; P. u Š. lu-kà-i-na-kum).[2] In the construction with two accusatives, one refers to the item contested and the other to the person considered liable and this is made clear in some examples quoted below by providing the personal name (if possible) with an accusative ending (printed bold in what follows). We find it in ICK 2, 118A:1-5, “ x refined silver D. will confirm for me (as the liability of) I. and Š.” (x kaspam ṣa-ru-pá-am D I-dí-a-ba-am u Š. ukânnam), which we may render by “to bring confirmation/proof for something against somebody”. It is also used in 98*:1-4, 1 kutānam Š. A-lá-ha-am ahušu ana Elamma uka’’an). The same construction occurs in  68*:16-20, where the disputed item is introduced by ana: A. has to provide Elamma with proof that not he, but two other persons have to pay for or deliver him textiles” (ana 11 kutānī  A. u B. ana Elamma  C. [subject] uka’’an). The result of such an action is described in 101*:7-9 by means of the G-stem: šumma ṣubātū iṣṣēr A (debtor) iktunnū, and here the liability is expressed by the preposition iṣṣēr, common in statements of debt (creditor iṣṣēr debtor išû). It can also be done by means of the preposition ina libbi , e.g. in 100*:6, šumma illibbi A uka’’an, “if he (Elamma) proves that A owes (them)” (cf. TC 2, 75:10-11, silver ša ina libbika tuka’’inanni) and by itti (KI), e.g. in BIN 4, 189:12-15, “H. will bring proof for me (that) x silver, the price of 3 textiles, is owed by H. “ (KI  H.  I. ukânnam).
        We also have the reciprocal Dt-stem, when in a case of disputed liability two parties must  try to confirm, prove each other’s liability, to find out who of them actually has to pay. Examples are given in CAD K, 171, kânu A, 5, where, however, the translations ”they acknowledged the indebtedness to each other” and “(silver) was debited to PN and to PN2 for mutual acknowledgment” are not correct. Both parties are ordered or invited to bring evidence in order to refute the other, so that it becomes clear who is really liable. This is clear from TC 3, 198:5-8, where the creditor declares: “As for the silver of U.’s son, K. and he will bring proof against each other and then I will collect my silver where it proves to be (owed) to me” (uktannūma / ašar ibūrranni / kaspī alaqqe). The verb buārum, “to appear, to prove to be”, shows that the purpose of kuta’’unum is to reveal the real debtor or creditor, The same verb[3] is used in CCT 2, 30:21-25, where the ownership of textiles deposited by the writer of this letter in the colonial office of Durhumit is at stake. The writer tells his addressee: “Let over there I1 and I2. bring proof against each other and where the textiles prove to be let them give my textiles to you” (I1 u I2 / lukta’’inūma / ašar ṣubātū iburrūninni / ṣubātēa liddinūnikkum). When in OIP 27, 62:10 silver is “laid upon” (nadi aṣṣēr) two men ana kuta’’unim they have to find (fight?) out who of them really owes them. This is nicely confirmed by our text *99:5-14, where two men must kuta’’unum to establish who has to pay for textiles and the one (A.) who proves to be liable for them (iṣṣēr A. iktūnu) will pay their price to Elamma.
        The presence of texts *98-101* and also of 68* in Elamma’s archive is understandable, since in four of them a claim of his is at stake. Proof of somebody’s liability has to be supplied “to Elamma” (68*:18 and *98:3) and in *100-*101 two persons have to establish (the Dt-stem) who of them has a liability to Elamma. Text *99 concerns a debt of Elamma, whose claim that a third person has collected it needs confirmation and it is natural that he kept (a copy of) this contract in his archive.

In both cases the envelope, the valid evidence to be consulted and by definition inscribed after the tablet had been placed inside it, has the earlier of the two consecutive eponymies. Comment
The only differences, apart from the fact that A adds the names of the fathers of the witnesses and the debtor, are that for A:12, “A. took it” (A. ilqe), B:11 (damaged) seems to have “they brought it to A.” (ana A. ublūnimma), and that for A:16, “its interest” (ṣibassu), B:18 writes “its accumulated interest” (ṣibātišu).
That this record of Elamma's liability (confirmed by his seal) was found in his archive, suggests that he managed to prove his case against Amur-Ištar, whereupon the record of this formal agreement must have been returned to him, after which, for reasons unknown to us, he must have decided not to destroy or discard it.

101. Kt 91/k 432 (1-285-91)


A witnessed record of formal arbitration resulting in a conditional a verdict passed by two(?) men, engaged by Elamma and Aššur-nādā to resolve their conflict. Elamma presumably had summoned Aššur-nādā to pay for textiles of his he had acquired, whereupon Aššur-nādā claimed that they had been taken by Ali-ahum, presumably Elamma’s brother. The verdict is procedural and obliges Aššur-nāda and Ali-ahum to establish by mutual oaths (kuta’’unum) who had taken them and has to pay Elamma. At the end the scribe, writing “these men are judges”, omitted the name of the second arbiter.

Notes
Note that the scribe consistently uses LA1.
15-18.The last lines show that more than one arbitrator, presumably two, were involved, so that the scribe by mistake must have omitted the name of the second one. Its mention in the list in OALP p. 321f. as the only case handled by one judge therefore can be corrected.  
Comment
The solution of the problem here is similar to that in ICK 1, 114, where Imdī-ilum confronts Kabiya, who denies that he owes a debt to Šu-Labān. Elamma and Aššur-nādā wish to end their conflict and seize two arbitrators (see for this procedure OALP 320-330), who give as verdict (dīššunu nidīn) that (lines 11-16) “if the 1 mina of silver is proved be owed by Kabiya to Šu-Labān” (šumma kaspum 1 mana illibbi Kabiya Šu-Labān ibtūram) he will pay Šu-Labān 1⅔ mina, hence the debt augmented by a heavy fine. The role Šu-Labān is unclear and the absence of ana before his name could mean that he is not mentioned as the beneficiary of the action, but as agent (“proved by him”), whose name precedes the verb; it would mean that the verb buārum, basically intransitive, would be transitive here. I assume that this text, using ina libbi to express the liability (as in 100*:6), tries to combine two different aspects, the procedure and its result: that Šu-Labān proves that Kabiya owes him the silver (kaspam illibbi K uka’’in) and that Kabiya is revealed as owing the silver (kaspum illibbi K. ibtūram), scil. to him. 


B. A conflict between Elamma and Pilah-Aššur (102-105)


The following four texts (two of which are virtual duplicates) deal with a conflict between Elamma and Pilah-Aššur about a commercial agreement. It must have led to a lawsuit, since three of the texts are records of testimonies by persons who had been “seized” by the parties to witness their meeting. They were recorded when these witnesses had been made to testify by and before the colony. How the conflict ended is not clear.

Comment on 102*-105*
It is not easy to reconstruct what happened, also because the identity of Pilah-Aššur (without patronym) is unknown. In the texts we meet at least eight different Pilah-Aššurs, in Elamma’s archive only the son of Dannum-ilī (*197:15), witness during the  year REL 90. Text 102* gives the best overview by offering the copies of two testimonies on two confrontations between Pilah-Aššur (P.) and Elamma (E.). From 103*:17-18 we know that P. “seized” three witnesses, Lāiqēp(um), Šu-Bēlum and Šarrum-Adad (the “us” of 104B*:3). 102* contains the testimony of Lā-qēp, son of Izzizam-ilī, and Šarrum-Adad stays out of the picture, but Šu-Bēlum is the subject of 105*. Here two friends of Elamma report on their seizure of Šu-Bēlum (no doubt at Elamma’s request), who then told them that he had already testified for Pilah-Aššur (when summoned in court) and proved so by producing the copies (mehrē, plural, l. 15) of his testimony, which confirmed that La-qēp had been with him (“L. was my fellow witness”, l. 18). Lines 20-27 then state that the writers by asking various people had confirmed Šu-Belum’s reliability. 102* must be of Šu-Bēlum, because it indeed contains two separate testimonies.[4] Moreover, Šu-Bēlum in 105*:12-14 asserts that the agreement between Pilah-Aššur and Elamma did not include the delivery of the silver in Assur, which is indeed not mentioned 102*, while it is in 104* and 105*. The latter then must be the testimony of Lā-qēp, the copy (mehram, singular) mentioned in 105*:6. Its original might have been 104*, sealed by Lā-qēp and still in its envelope.
        According to the first testimony in 102* Pilah-Aššur confronted Elamma, when he was about to depart on a journey to Assur (cf. lines 15-16, “when I return from the City”), with his need for tin, either because he had to offer tin to Lā-qēp (as his qīptum?) or because the latter had failed to give him tin. In both cases the credit term was very long (ca. 16 months), which must have been attractive for the recipient. Because Lā-qēp belonged to his firm or family Elamma feels that he has to offer Pilah-Aššur his own tin, but he asks a reasonable price,[5] payable within 20 weeks, which is a much shorter term. Pilah-Aššur then asks Elamma a favor in return and the latter promises to give him on his return from Assur (perhaps after he had sold the merchandise imported from there) 15 minas of silver, which Pilah-Aššur can send to Assur to make purchases. He will have full disposal of (be’ālum) the merchandise purchased with it, no doubt to sell it in such a way that he can pay back the 15 minas given him by Elamma and can earn money for himself.  
        The reconstruction is complicated because 103* (Lā-qēp’s testimony) offers a different picture. Here Pilah-Aššur - not referring to what is reported in 102*:4-8, but only to Elamma’s offer of tin as something of the past (taddin, l. 6) - reminds Elamma of his promise of putting silver at his disposal for making purchases in Assur (cf. 102*:14-21), which Elamma then confirms. But he adapts Elamma’s promise to his own advantage: not when the latter returned from Assur (as stated in 102*:15-16), but when he himself wants to go there. Moreover, according 102*:18-21, Elamma had offered him the silver so that he could send it to Assur, while here Pilah-Aššur himself intends to bring it to Assur (to make purchases with it). An again different picture is offered by the second testimony in 104*:22-37, for it states that Pilah-Aššur had given Elamma, again when he was about to depart for Assur, 15 minas of silver (plus the necessary transport costs), asking Elamma to buy with it tin and textiles for him in Assur, to be sent to Kanesh with the first opportunity; this Elamma promised. I cannot believe that the two encounters and testimonies, copied on one single tablet, are not related and that two different journeys of Elamma to Assur are meant. The reformulation in 103*:11-13, where “I will give you to send to Assur” is changed into “you will give me when I go to Assur”, makes me assume that the second testimony of 102* reflects still another change: Elamma had given Pilah-Aššur the silver promised, but instead of sending it or going himself to Assur, Pilah-Aššur now asks Elamma to take it along himself and to carry out purchases for him (which he describes: tin and textiles), a request accepted by Elamma. The purpose of the granting of silver, making purchases in Assur, anyhow would be realized.
        The fact that all four texts record or mention confrontations and that the testimonies were given in the framework of a lawsuit before the colony, implies there had been problems, which must have been related to the change of plans by Pilah-Aššur. To solve them it was necessary to establish what had actually been promised and arranged and what had changed, which was done by asking testimonies from the persons who had been present as witnesses. It seems likely that the arrangements between Elamma and Pilah-Aššur had not been immediately recorded in writing and had to be reconstructed from memory. Ideally there should be a joint testimony by all three witnesses in agreement (šībū ētamdūtum), for which purpose the individual testimonies – written down as drafts or narrated – had to be coordinated (see Veenhof 2003a, 95 with note 39). This had not (or not yet) been done in this case. The testimony of Lā-qēp in 103*:17-19 mentions the other two witnesses, but they were apparently not available when he was put on the stand in the colony. Text 103* is very similar to 104* (still in its unopened envelope), but there is a difference: in 103* Lā-qēp states “I gave my testimony before the dagger of Assur”, but in 104* “I gave the tablet of the gate of the god”. The first is a record of the testimony given under oath during the lawsuit, the second may have been a tablet recorded earlier or elsewhere, which Lā-qēp simply handed in. The letter 105*:5-6 mentions that Elamma’s friends (in Kanesh) had available “the tablet, a copy, of Lā-qēp” and confronted the second witness, Šu-Bēlum, with it in order to hear his testimony, i.e. whether he could confirm what Lā-qēp had recorded. There proved to be a difference, for while La-qēp had stated that the first arrangement had included the promise that Pilah-Aššur should deliver the silver to Elamma in Assur, Šu-Bēlum asserted that “delivering (the silver) in Assur had not been said”. It must have meant that Pilah-Aššur could give the silver to Elamma in Kanesh and thus would not have to bear the costs of shipping it to Assur (the import duty and the transport fee). To prove his point he showed them the copies of his own testimony, which mentioned that Lā-qēp had been with him when they attended the encounter, which meant that he spoke the truth. The writers of 105*, confronted with this difference, then were able to establish, by questioning colleagues, that Šu-Bēlum was a reliable person and had a good record. This implied that, notwithstanding Lā-qēp’s claim, there was no obligation for Pilah-Aššur to ship the silver to Elamma in Assur. This must have been one of the reasons for the problems arisen and it may have made Elamma hesitate to meet his promise. It is remarkable that 102*:26-29 mentions that when, eventually, Pilah-Aššur asked Elamma to take the silver (that he had originally given him) to Assur, it is said to include the import duty and transport fee, so that Elamma would not incur additional costs. 
        We do not know how the problem was solved, for Elamma’s archive does not contain a relevant verdict of the colony or evidence of further cooperation between the two men. One wonders why only these records were preserved in Elamma’s archive and no documentation about a solution.

The City usually decided that nobody should take or “touch” (ṭahā’um, “approach”) anything, because all assets had to be gathered and evaluated in Assur in view of a regular liquidation; who prematurely seized assets would be considered to have stolen them. See for such verdicts Veenhof 1995, 1725-27, and also AKT 6-a, 294:19-22, where the prohibition to take anything “in the countryside” (ina eqlim), in Anatolia, matches ammakam, “over there”, in l. 1 of our text. Unfortunately we do not know whose death was the cause of this verdict.

A testament


120. Kt 91/k 396 (1-250-91)


The testament of Iddin-Aššur, son of Ilī-dān, in its unfortunately unopened envelope, which only records the prohibition (addressed to the heirs) to make the testamentary executioners  swear an oath, no doubt in an attempt to fight the stipulations. It was already published in Veenhof 2012a, 173-174, as text 1.

Notes
1. The testator thus far does not occur with his patronym in other texts and the contents of the texts on whose envelopes seal A appears are unknown. But the inscription on the bulla Kt a/k 1264, sealed by him, [KIŠIB k]à-ri-im / Kà-ni-iš, shows that he once acted as representative of the colony.[6] He should be someone with relations with Elamma and could be identical to Iddin-Aššur, who wrote the letters *31 to Elamma and Kurub-Ištar, *32 to Kurub-Ištar and tamkārum, and *92 to Elamma and Ali-ahum. 
6. The presence of this last will in Elamma's archive may be due to the fact that he was one of the testamentary executors (bēl šimātim), in which case there would be other copies in the archives of his two colleagues), or it may have been deposited in his house for safe-keeping.
9-10. See for this expression, which implies that the person “led down” had to swear an oath  before or by the emblem of the god Aššur, OALP 196-7.
Comment
The first sealer is the testator himself, as is the case in ICK 1, 12a, where he also the first one. In the two other testaments (Kt c/k 843a, courtesy of J.G. Dercksen, and Kt. o/k 196, ArAn 4, 2000, 21; see the photo in Anatolia’s Prologue 338 no. 443), preserved with their envelopes, the testator is not mentioned as such. This is surprising, since a testament is recorded as a literal quotation (in the first person singular) of what the testator had declared, which one would expect him to validate by his seal impression. But one could argue that the seals of the testamentary witnesses/executors were sufficient proof that what is recorded is indeed the testator’s last will.
I assume that the three persons mentioned in lines 2-5 are the bēl šimātim, probably acting both as "executors" and as "witnesses to the testamentary disposition" (see Veenhof 2012a, 178). It is a pity that no permission was obtained to open this envelope, so that the contents of such a rare and important document remain unknown.
