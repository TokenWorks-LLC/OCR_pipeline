That it qualifies the payment for the 3 textiles of “your temple fund” as due by the colony” (ša li-bi4  kà-ri-im) probably means that it had charged them together with the other textiles, while as “temple fund” they were exempt from taxation.         ta-na-pá-al                18 shekels of silver.”


        These records reflect the accounting of the colony, which concerned the substantial annual contribution, called dātum, which important traders, called “dātum-payers”, owed to the colony in exchange for certain privileges (see the description in OAI 125ff.). The “shares (qātum) of the traders were “deposited” (nadā’um) on several occasions, called “pouring” (šitapkum), by paying amounts of silver or depositing textiles, which were then sold by the organization, presumably to the local palaces. The book value of a trader’s deposits in the colony was registered and at the time of the annual accounting (nikkassū), after the size of that year’s dātum had been fixed, it was calculated how much a trader still had to pay in addition to the deposits already made (OAI ch. 11). Our texts record an itemized settlement of Hinnāya (already discussed in OAI 226), which summarizes what he had already contributed and what still had to be paid (napālum) to meet his obligation. 
        The total of the assets, 25 minas 15 shekels, consists of 23 minas 20 shekels of silver already deposited, to which are added amounts for various lots of textiles, including a few of the addressee’s “temple fund”, which, as B is helpful in adding, are owed to him by the colony Adding the balancing payment still to be made (lines 12-13) would yield a dātum amounting to 26 ½ minas of silver or 27 minas according to Kt m/k 91. The final lines of B concern different (“separately”) small claims and debts.
        In the texts two persons appear: 1) the speaker “I”, explicit in A:14 and B:13, 18, who  must be the person addressing “you” in A; 2) Hinnāya, whose account is the subject (A-B:2, A:14-15) and who must be identical to the “you”, because of A:10-14, for he is the one who had made the deposits and has to make balance payments to the colony and (B:21) to Elamma. “I” must have been a trader representing the absent Hinnāya at the accounting and reports its outcome to him (“you”). Dercksen, OAI 226, suggests that it may have been Pūšu-kēn, but that is difficult to prove and the presence of 122* in Elamma’s archive suggests that he is speaking. That he is mentioned in B:16 and 19 is not a problem, since this is not a letter and the few items dealing with Elamma are added at the end, perhaps to update the memo. Elamma as writer fits his close contacts with Hinnāya, who seems to have been his partner.         
        Text 121*, also about the accounting of the kārum, is related to 122* and deals with the dātum contributions of Elamma and of Šu-Kūbum. Here the balance payment by Šu-Kūbum, 2 minas 10 shekels (lines 13-14), added to his deposit of in all 24 5/6 minas, yields a total of 27 minas, the same figure as deduced from 122*. This suggests that Elamma’s dātum had the same size, which implies that to the total of lines 1-6, 25 minas 42 ½ shekels, a balance payment of 1 mina 17 ½ shekels has to be made, but according to lines 19-21 he has to pay only 42 shekels, because (small) balance payments already made for him by Hinnāya and Šu-Kūbum (lines 15-18) are deducted. How to restore 121*:10-12 is unclear. The amount of l. 9 might be the balance payment due by Hinnāya according to 122*:12-13 and be meant in lines 15-16. Anyhow, we have to distinguish balance payments to be made to the colony in order to arrive at the required amount of dātum, and balance payments among the traders who helped or represented each other. All in all it seems very likely that 121* and 122* refer to the same accounting, based on a dātum of 27 minas of silver per trader, whereby the first text focuses on the accounts of Elamma and his partner Šu-Kūbum and the second on that of Hinnāya, who had been represented by Elamma.

Comment 
The amount of tin and the average price of textiles and donkeys suggests a load of two donkeys. What Elamma did is called sanāqum, basically “to check”, but with a dative suffix the verb is also used for to hand over while checking and weighing or counting. Elamma acquired the ownership of this lot, probably part of a larger caravan, from the writers via a transaction that must have been arranged or prepared in Assur, because the price of the tin is the one current there, which is confirmed by the mention of “loose tin”, always made available in Assur to transporters of a caravan. The payment for the donkeys and their load must have taken place in Kanesh, when it was delivered to him. The identity of writers remains unknown, but the presence of this record in the archive suggests they were associated with his firm.

Where this is to happen is not mentioned, it could be with the ruler of the town in whose territory the losses had occurred and who by treaty was responsible for compensation, or perhaps to the City Assembly in Assur, which could pass a verdict about the sharing of the losses of such a caravan, as is clear from the verdict Kt n/k 1570, discussed in Veenhof 1995, 1731 and taken up again in OAI 172.

Comments on *142 and *143
We have two more lists of the distribution of jars with beer (kirrum), APU 324 = CCT 5, 42a and 364 = RA 81 (1987) 16 no. 5, in which a number of persons each receive one jar, apart from two cases of two in CCT 5, 42a. Expenses for such jars with beer (usually “I paid ... for a kirrum”) are mentioned repeatedly in OA texts (cf. CAD K 409f., 2, a). Bread lists are more numerous and those known previously were edited as APU 309, 314, 334, 338-340. Additional lists are Kt c/k 180 (courtesy Dercksen), with eight entries, and Kt f/k 188 (courtesy L. Umur; beginning rev. destroyed), with at least 24 entries; they list in all at least ca. 2500 breads. In most of these lists people receive 50 or 100 breads, with a few cases of 40 or 60 (APU 334: 31, 35, 38; 338:6; 339:3) and twice 200 (Kt f/k 188:6-7). The lists suggests that their writers (in our case presumably Ali-ahum) supplied them to the persons mentioned. In Kt f/k 188 (presumably from the archive of Peruwa) several of the recipients have Anatolian names (e.g. Balhaziya, Šašahšušar, Šuhurpiya). The slightly damaged list ICK 1, 181 (APU 334), which also mentions several Anatolians among the recipients, also lists other items, presumably bakery products (tahšimam and kukam tahšimam, also in Kt c/k 95: 2, after bread), beer, wine, footwear and jars with beer, and APU 340 also a few shekels of silver alongside the bread. Our lists can be compared with those that register that a large number of people each receive a kind of sheep (lakannum, šalinnum, in APU no. 370), alongside amounts of 5 or 6 minas of wool.[1]
        One could consider such lists records of expenses for a meal or a drinking party, but when whole sheep are distributed it must be either a special occasion or a measure to provide people with meat rations. This last interpretation is also suggested by the large numbers of breads, 1350 in our text, even 3080 in APU 334, which the recipients could at best consume over a long  period of time, redistribute or take along on a caravan journey. The numbers of breads and vessels with beer are too big to assume that they were prepared by and in the house of one prominent trader, e.g. for the members of his household, his personnel, agents, and colleagues. It is much more likely that such a trader had ordered and acquired them from a local bakery and brewery.
        While most lists, acquired on the antiques market, cannot be connected with a particular archive, ICK 1, 181 (APU 334) was excavated by Hrozný in “locus 4”, which means that it belonged to the archives of Imdī-ilum. The study of the persons appearing in this text, who certainly did not all belong to his household or firm, has to wait for a reconstruction of his archive. But I note that among the recipients are well-known traders, such as Elamma and Pūšu-kēn (lines 6 and 34, for respectively 50 and 100 breads), which shows that also friends and colleagues were supplied. It also includes two deliveries to “the household of PN” (lines 8-9). In *142 several of the  twenty recipients of bread are well-known from other texts of the archive - e.g. Amur-Ištar, Aššur-rē’ī, Aššur-ṣulūlī, Aninum, La-qēp, Kurub-Ištar, and Šu-Kūbum -  and it probably includes personnel, business partners or relatives (the last one is “my brother’s son”). Aššur-imittī, son of Halābum, appears in *70:4 as recipient of a be’ūlātu-loan, which identifies him as a member of Elamma’s personnel. In other cases identification is difficult due to the lack of patronyms, but we also meet persons who certainly did not belong to Elamma’s family or firm, such as Imdī-ilim (l. 11), an important, independent trader, and Tupizi, who receives 50 breads in APU 334:26 and 100 breads in Kt c/k 180, a list that also mentions Pūšu-kēn and Šu-Kūbum, both as recipients of  100 breads. In Kt f/k 188 several of the recipients as designated “the wife of PN” (of Iliš-tikal, Zurzur, Hanu, Arawa and I-na-[x (x)]). 
        The lists show that a trader – apparently a prominent one, such as Elamma or his brother, Ali-ahum, and Imdīlum - acquired large quantities of bread and beer to supply them to others, possibly including his personnel, but also his business associates and colleagues. The anon-ymous writer of the short memo Kt c/k 95 (courtesy Dercksen) states that he had 100 and 50 breads brought (šēbulum) to other men. The breads are not gifts, since the list APU 314:13-15 ends with the words: “All these men are (hereby) indebted to me” (mimma awīlē anniuttim habbulūnim) and the fact that the size of the debt is not mentioned may be explained by assuming that breads had a standard price. This makes such lists memorandums of claims by the writer on the recipients, but they do not mention why a trader provided this service. Did they take turns in doing so or was it a commercial enterprise, which allowed the trader in question to earn money as a kind of whole-sale supplier? The existence of such a service fits the specific nature of an Assyrian colony, which comprised many men who lived and worked without women to care for their daily needs and many people involved in the trade who visited it (caravaneers, transporters, messengers) did not have houses of their own. They had to be helped by others, possibly their employers or associates or simply merchants engaged “catering services”, who provided the food and drinks they needed.

Comment
The absence of a preposition (e.g.  ana, “for”, as in APU 332, or  išti, “due by”, e.g. in APU 320), leaves the purpose of the list unclear, as is the case in APU 322, 359 and 365 (where a few amounts are described as qātum/ qādum ša ṣuhārim, “share of / including that of the boy”). It could record a distribution of small gifts to the persons mentioned or gifts by them. The former interpretation is unlikely, because most persons are not well-known from Elamma’s archive. The latter is suggested by the subscript of APU 367:19-20 (a list of 17 amounts of silver, mostly all ranging from 1 to 2 shekels), “All this they donated to my amtum”, where amtum probably is the wife of a trader. The gifts might have been made on the occasion of her marriage or the birth of a child.
